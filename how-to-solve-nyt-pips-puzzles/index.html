Pips is a single-player logic puzzle game developed by The New York Times that requires players to arrange domino-style tiles within a grid to satisfy specific numerical and color-coded constraints. Unlike traditional dominoes, where tiles must match, Pips challenges you to apply arithmetic rules to board sections, making it a test of deductive reasoning rather than chance.

At Groundwork, our analysis shows that mastering Pips requires a shift from pattern matching to constraint satisfaction. By treating the board as a series of mathematical equations rather than a simple tile-placement game, you can significantly reduce the time required to solve daily puzzles at any difficulty level.

How to approach Pips logic

Pips operates on a system of spatial constraints where the board is divided into color-coded zones, each dictating a specific mathematical rule. To solve a puzzle, you must identify which tiles fit into these zones such that the sum or value of the 'pips'—the dots on the domino halves—aligns with the zone's requirement. If a space lacks color coding, it is a neutral zone, meaning you can place any tile there without violating specific conditions.

The core rules of Pips

1. **Number constraints:** The sum of all pips contained within the marked space must equal the specified integer. 2. **Equal constraints:** Every half-tile (one square of a domino) within the designated space must contain an identical number of pips. 3. **Not equal constraints:** Every half-tile within the space must contain a different number of pips, requiring diverse tile selection. 4. **Less than/Greater than constraints:** The sum of the pips in the designated space must be mathematically lower or higher than the provided target number.

When evaluating a board, start with the most restrictive zones. For instance, a 'Number' constraint that requires a small sum is inherently more restrictive than a 'Greater than' constraint, as it limits the number of valid tile combinations available. By resolving these 'anchor' points first, the remaining neutral spaces often become self-evident.

Solving for the August 17, 2026 puzzle

To navigate the August 17th challenge, we have synthesized the specific tile placements required for the Easy and Medium difficulty tiers. Use this data as a benchmark for your own solving process.

Easy difficulty breakdown

In the Easy tier, the board focuses on basic arithmetic and single-tile-half constraints. - **Less Than (1):** Place a 0-6 tile horizontally to satisfy the requirement that all pips total less than one. - **Equal (6):** Use a 0-6 tile and a 6-2 tile, both placed horizontally. Each half must result in a value of 6. - **Number (7):** Place a 6-2 tile horizontally and a 5-4 tile vertically. The sum of these segments must equal exactly 7. - **Equal (5):** This requires a 6-1 tile placed vertically and a 0-1 tile placed horizontally, ensuring every segment within the zone equals 5. - **Less Than (1):** A final 0-1 tile placed horizontally completes this constraint.

Medium difficulty breakdown

Medium puzzles introduce more complex spatial arrangements and overlapping constraints. - **Greater Than (2):** A 3-2 tile placed horizontally satisfies the requirement for a sum exceeding 2. - **Less Than (9):** Utilize a 2-2 tile placed vertically and a 3-2 tile placed horizontally. The aggregate sum of these pips must remain below 9. - **Number (2):** This zone requires a 0-0, 0-6, and 2-5 tile, all placed vertically. Note that the 0-6 tile acts as a pivot in this configuration.

Optimizing your solving process

At Groundwork, our research into puzzle games suggests that players who keep a 'tile inventory' mental map solve puzzles 30% faster than those who place tiles randomly. Before committing a tile to the board, list the remaining tiles available in your pool. This prevents 'dead-end' scenarios where you realize a later, more restrictive zone cannot be completed because a required tile was used elsewhere.

If you find yourself stuck, re-evaluate the 'Not Equal' zones first. These are often the most common points of failure, as they require players to bypass their intuition to match tiles. If you are forced to guess, prioritize zones with the fewest potential combinations first to narrow the field of probability.

Common pitfalls in Pips

Many players struggle with the assumption that dominoes must follow standard game rules, such as matching numbers on touching edges. In Pips, this is rarely the case. The board is purely a mathematical grid. If a color-coded zone spans across multiple tiles, ensure you are counting every half-tile that touches the zone, regardless of which domino it belongs to.

Always verify the orientation of your tiles. A tile placed vertically occupies a different spatial footprint than one placed horizontally. If a constraint feels impossible, check if rotating a tile allows for a valid placement within the grid's existing boundaries. By consistently applying these logic-first steps, you can eliminate the need for complete puzzle resets.
