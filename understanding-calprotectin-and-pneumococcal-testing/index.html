Understanding new medical diagnostics: calprotectin and pneumococcal tests

By Dr. Sarah Jenkins • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Learn how the new calprotectin stool test and pneumococcal disease tests help doctors diagnose inflammation and bacterial infections accurately.

Outline

  • What is the calprotectin stool test?
  • How do pneumococcal disease tests work?
  • Why do diagnostic tests change over time?
  • How to evaluate medical information online

Medical diagnostic tests are clinical procedures used to identify the presence, absence, or severity of a disease or condition in a patient. The National Library of Medicine’s MedlinePlus platform recently updated its database to include comprehensive information on two critical diagnostic tools: the calprotectin stool test and pneumococcal disease tests. Understanding how these tests function is essential for patients navigating gastrointestinal or respiratory health concerns.

According to the National Library of Medicine, diagnostic accuracy relies on selecting the appropriate test based on clinical symptoms rather than generalized screening (MedlinePlus, 2024). When your physician orders a test, they are looking for specific biomarkers that reveal underlying physiological changes.

What is the calprotectin stool test?

A calprotectin stool test is a diagnostic procedure that measures the levels of calprotectin, a protein released by white blood cells, within a stool sample. Elevated concentrations of this protein in the digestive tract typically serve as a reliable biomarker for intestinal inflammation, often helping clinicians differentiate between inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and non-inflammatory conditions like irritable bowel syndrome (IBS).

When you experience persistent diarrhea or abdominal discomfort, your healthcare provider may order this test to assess the degree of inflammation in your intestines. Because calprotectin is stable in stool, it provides a non-invasive way to monitor disease activity in patients already diagnosed with conditions such as Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis. Research indicates that low levels of fecal calprotectin have a high negative predictive value, meaning a normal result can often rule out active organic inflammation, thereby reducing the need for more invasive procedures like colonoscopies in certain patient populations (Crohn’s & Colitis Foundation, 2023).

How do pneumococcal disease tests work?

Pneumococcal disease tests are a group of diagnostic assays designed to detect the presence of *Streptococcus pneumoniae* bacteria in various bodily fluids, including blood, urine, sputum, or cerebrospinal fluid. These tests confirm bacterial infections that can lead to pneumonia, meningitis, or bloodstream infections, allowing for the targeted administration of antibiotics.

The choice of sample depends on the suspected location of the infection. For instance, if your doctor suspects pneumonia, they may request a sputum culture to analyze the mucus you cough up from your lungs. If they suspect a systemic infection or meningitis, they may collect blood or cerebrospinal fluid to identify the specific bacterial strain. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), identifying the specific pathogen is critical for managing antibiotic resistance, as different strains of *S. pneumoniae* exhibit varying levels of susceptibility to common treatments (CDC, 2024).

Why do diagnostic tests change over time?

Diagnostic protocols evolve as medical research advances, leading to the introduction of more precise testing methods and the retirement of outdated procedures. MedlinePlus regularly updates its medical encyclopedia to reflect these changes, ensuring that patients have access to evidence-based information regarding how their tests are performed and what the results signify for their long-term health.

Staying informed about your diagnostic tests involves three primary steps: 1. Ask your physician about the clinical necessity of the test: Ensure you understand why the test is being performed and what the results will change regarding your treatment plan. 2. Review the procedural requirements: Some tests, like stool samples, require specific collection techniques to ensure the sample is not contaminated, which could lead to inaccurate results. 3. Discuss the limitations: No diagnostic test is 100% accurate. Ask your doctor about the potential for false positives or false negatives, especially if your symptoms persist despite a "normal" result.

How to evaluate medical information online

Accessing accurate medical information is a core component of patient agency. As the medical landscape becomes increasingly complex, the ability to discern credible sources from misinformation is vital. The National Library of Medicine emphasizes that health information should always be corroborated by peer-reviewed research or established government health agencies (MedlinePlus, 2024).

When evaluating medical websites, consider the following: - Check for evidence-based citations: Does the article link to primary sources, such as clinical trials or government health departments? - Verify the date of publication: Medical guidelines change frequently; ensure you are reading the most recent version of the information. - Look for transparency: Reliable sites will clearly state their editorial process and identify who wrote or reviewed the content for medical accuracy.

By leveraging resources like MedlinePlus, you can gain a foundational understanding of the tests your doctor requests. While this information does not replace clinical judgment, it empowers you to have more productive conversations with your healthcare team, ensuring that you are an active participant in your diagnostic and treatment journey.
