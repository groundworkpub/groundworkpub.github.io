Federal Reserve interest rate outlook and market impact

By Groundwork Editorial • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Is the Fed raising rates in September? Groundwork analyzes the latest economic data and institutional forecasts to explain the outlook for interest rates.

In This Guide

  • Why are rate hikes unlikely in the coming months?
  • How do interest rates influence risk-on asset markets?
  • What should investors monitor during FOMC cycles?
  • Is the current economic data signaling a recession or a soft landing?

A Federal Reserve interest rate decision is the central bank's adjustment of the federal funds rate, which dictates the cost of borrowing for banks and indirectly influences all consumer and corporate lending. When the Fed signals a pause or a decrease in rates, it typically signals a shift toward more accommodative monetary policy, which can increase liquidity in financial markets.

At Groundwork, our analysis shows that current market indicators and institutional forecasts, including recent assessments from Goldman Sachs, suggest that a September interest rate increase is highly improbable. This shift in sentiment is driven by cooling inflation data and softening economic indicators, which reduce the pressure on the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) to maintain a restrictive policy stance.

Why are rate hikes unlikely in the coming months?

A rate hike is unlikely in the near term because recent economic reports indicate that inflation is moderating toward the Fed’s 2% target, and labor market data suggests a cooling in consumer demand. When inflation slows, the Fed faces less pressure to maintain elevated borrowing costs to dampen economic activity.

Goldman Sachs chief economist Jan Hatzius noted that current market pricing for the federal funds rate remains overly hawkish, meaning investors are anticipating more aggressive tightening than the underlying economic data justifies. According to data from the CME FedWatch tool, the probability of a 25-basis-point increase has dropped significantly, with the majority of market participants now expecting the status quo to persist.

This shift is supported by: 1. Sustained slowing in core inflation metrics. 2. Weaker-than-expected retail sales, signaling a contraction in consumer spending. 3. A stabilization in employment figures, indicating that the labor market is no longer overheating.

How do interest rates influence risk-on asset markets?

Interest rates serve as the primary lever for global liquidity, where higher rates increase the cost of capital and lower rates incentivize investment in riskier assets. When the Federal Reserve raises rates, the cost of borrowing rises, which typically draws capital away from speculative or volatile assets toward safer, yield-bearing instruments like Treasury bonds.

At Groundwork, our research framework highlights that the relationship between monetary policy and asset performance is inverse. During periods of aggressive tightening, such as the 2022 Fed cycle, capital flight from equities and digital assets was pronounced. Conversely, the expectation of a "pivot"—where the Fed transitions from hiking to holding or cutting rates—often acts as a catalyst for market recovery. For assets like Bitcoin, which are highly sensitive to global fiat liquidity, the removal of the threat of higher rates is viewed as a significant tailwind.

What should investors monitor during FOMC cycles?

To navigate interest rate uncertainty, investors should focus on the "dot plot" projections, FOMC meeting minutes, and the specific language used in post-meeting press conferences. These documents provide the most direct insight into the committee’s internal debate regarding the path of monetary policy.

Follow these steps to evaluate your portfolio against potential rate changes: 1. Monitor the CME FedWatch tool weekly to see how market sentiment shifts in response to incoming economic data. 2. Review the FOMC minutes released three weeks after every meeting to identify if dissent is growing among voting members. 3. Analyze core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation data, as this is the primary metric the Fed uses to determine the necessity of future rate adjustments. 4. Stress-test your high-growth or speculative positions to ensure they can withstand a "higher-for-longer" interest rate environment, even if current expectations lean toward a pause.

Is the current economic data signaling a recession or a soft landing?

The debate between a recession and a soft landing centers on whether the Fed can reduce inflation without triggering a significant contraction in GDP and employment. A soft landing occurs when inflation returns to target levels while economic growth remains positive, whereas a recession is marked by a sustained, broad-based decline in economic activity.

Recent data suggests a nuanced picture. While retail sales have softened, employment remains relatively resilient. At Groundwork, our synthesis of economic benchmarks suggests that the current environment is consistent with a "normalization" phase. This means the economy is moving away from the post-pandemic volatility toward a more sustainable, long-term growth trajectory. However, the risk remains that if the Fed holds rates at restrictive levels for too long, the cumulative effect on credit availability could tip the economy into a recessionary period. Investors should prepare for volatility in either scenario by maintaining a diversified asset allocation that is not overly dependent on a single outcome for interest rates.
