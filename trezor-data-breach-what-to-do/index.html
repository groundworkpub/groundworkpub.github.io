What to do if you were affected by the Trezor data breach

By Marcus Vance • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Trezor confirmed a data breach at its shipping partner, ShipMonk. Here is what was stolen, why your crypto is safe, and how to avoid phishing scams.

Outline

  • Was your cryptocurrency wallet compromised by this breach?
  • What specific information was exposed?
  • How can you protect yourself from phishing attempts?
  • What should you do if you were notified by Trezor?
  • Why are third-party breaches so common?

A third-party data breach is an incident where sensitive information stored by a company's service provider is accessed by unauthorized parties. In the recent case involving Trezor, a fulfillment partner named ShipMonk experienced a security failure that exposed the personal contact and shipping details of approximately 14,000 customers. This breach does not mean your cryptocurrency funds are compromised, but it does increase the likelihood of targeted phishing attempts against you.

According to data from cybersecurity firm SentinelOne, global data breaches have risen by 17% in 2026 compared to the previous year, with an average of 2,090 attacks occurring worldwide each week. This incident serves as a critical reminder that your security is only as strong as the weakest link in the supply chain.

Was your cryptocurrency wallet compromised by this breach?

No, your cryptocurrency remains secure because the breach occurred at a third-party shipping partner, not within Trezor’s own infrastructure. The security of a hardware wallet relies on the device itself and your private keys, which are never transmitted to or stored by shipping companies. The information leaked was limited to "off-chain" data, meaning your digital assets were never exposed or at risk of unauthorized access through this specific event.

What specific information was exposed?

The compromised data varies depending on the customer, but it generally includes personally identifiable information (PII) that makes you a target for social engineering. For 11,742 customers, the leak included full names, email addresses, phone numbers, and physical shipping addresses. An additional 1,947 customers had their names, cities, and email addresses exposed. This data is the primary material used by attackers to craft convincing phishing campaigns, which often involve impersonating reputable companies or support staff to gain your trust.

How can you protect yourself from phishing attempts?

You must treat every unexpected communication claiming to be from Trezor, a bank, or a crypto exchange with extreme skepticism. Because attackers now possess your physical address, phone number, and email, they can launch "multi-channel" attacks. For example, you might receive a text message followed by a phone call from someone claiming to be a security professional from a wallet manufacturer, asking you to "verify" your seed phrase or move funds to a "safe" address.

To protect yourself, follow these steps: 1. Never share your seed phrase: No legitimate company, including Trezor, will ever ask for your 12-to-24-word recovery phrase or your PIN under any circumstances. 2. Ignore unsolicited contacts: If you receive a call, email, or physical letter regarding your crypto security, do not use the provided contact details. Instead, visit the official website by typing the URL directly into your browser. 3. Enable two-factor authentication: Ensure all your email and financial accounts have strong, unique passwords and multi-factor authentication (MFA) enabled, preferably using an authenticator app rather than SMS. 4. Use a burner email: For future purchases, consider using a dedicated email address or a P.O. box if the vendor allows, to limit the amount of personal data tied to your high-value hardware purchases.

What should you do if you were notified by Trezor?

If you received an email notification from Trezor, you should assume your contact information is currently circulating in databases used by scammers. While there is no immediate action required to "fix" the breach, you must heighten your vigilance. Monitor your email and phone for suspicious activity, and be prepared to delete any message that requests your personal login credentials or financial information.

It is also worth noting that this breach follows a trend of similar incidents affecting major crypto hardware providers. Ledger, a primary competitor, suffered a massive database leak in 2020 that resulted in years of sustained phishing and extortion attempts against its customer base. By understanding that this is a long-term risk, you can stay ahead of attackers who rely on the element of surprise.

Why are third-party breaches so common?

Companies often outsource logistics, manufacturing, and marketing to specialized third-party firms to improve efficiency and reduce overhead costs. However, every time you share your data with an organization, you are also implicitly sharing it with all of their vendors. This creates a "third-party risk" that is often difficult to audit. When a partner like ShipMonk is breached, the primary company—in this case, Trezor—is often left to manage the fallout, even though their own internal systems remained secure.

As a consumer, you cannot prevent these breaches, but you can minimize the impact by compartmentalizing your digital footprint. Use unique, complex passwords for every service and remain aware that your shipping information is essentially "public" data in the eyes of cybercriminals. If you are ever in doubt about a message, always contact the company through their verified, official support channels before taking any action.
