Pips is a single-player logic puzzle game from the New York Times that applies domino-placement mechanics to color-coded grid constraints. Unlike traditional dominoes where you match identical values, Pips requires you to arrange tiles so that the segments within specific color-coded zones satisfy mathematical conditions, such as sums, equality, or inequality requirements.

How does Pips work?

Pips is a grid-based puzzle where you place domino tiles either horizontally or vertically to fill designated spaces on a board. A Pips tile functions identically to a standard domino, featuring two ends with a specific number of pips (dots) ranging from zero to six. The core challenge lies in navigating the color-coded regions that overlay the grid. Each region imposes a unique mathematical rule that every tile segment touching that zone must obey. If a space lacks color coding, it functions as a neutral area with no placement restrictions, allowing you to use it for overflow or to connect tiles that must satisfy constraints elsewhere on the board.

What are the common Pips constraints?

To succeed in Pips, you must interpret the shorthand rules assigned to each color-coded zone. These rules dictate how the pips within that space must relate to one another or to a target number. According to the New York Times game mechanics (NYT Games, 2025), the most frequent constraints you will encounter include:

1. **Number:** The sum of all pips located inside this color-coded area must equal the specific number provided. 2. **Equal:** Every individual domino half within this space must display the exact same number of pips. 3. **Not Equal:** Every domino half situated in this space must contain a different number of pips from the others. 4. **Less than:** The total value of every domino half in this area must be less than the target number. 5. **Greater than:** Every domino half within this space must add up to more than the target number.

It is important to note that a single domino tile can span across two different zones. If a tile crosses a boundary, only the half of the domino residing within the color-coded space is subject to that specific zone’s rule. This mechanic is essential for solving more complex puzzles where you must balance multiple conflicting constraints simultaneously.

How to approach a Pips puzzle

When you start a new Pips board, begin by identifying the most restrictive color-coded zones first. These are typically the areas that demand a specific sum or an 'Equal' constraint, as they significantly limit the number of tiles that can legally occupy those spaces.

1. **Identify the constraints:** Scan the board for specific numbers and rules. Prioritize 'Equal' zones, as they often force you to use specific doubles or tiles with the same values. 2. **Map the board:** Look for areas where tiles must overlap multiple zones. A tile placed to satisfy a 'Number' constraint in one zone might inadvertently violate an 'Equal' constraint in another. 3. **Work from the edges:** If the board layout permits, place tiles in corners or dead-end spaces first to reduce the number of variables you have to track. 4. **Check for parity:** If you are playing a harder level, remember that there are a finite number of dominoes in a standard set. If you find yourself needing a 6-6 tile but you have already used it, you must backtrack.

Why do I keep getting stuck?

Because Pips is a logic-based game, getting stuck usually indicates that an earlier placement has created a contradiction. If you reach a point where no available tile can satisfy a zone’s constraint, you likely made an error in a previous step. Unlike word games like Wordle, where each guess is independent, Pips is a holistic system where the position of every tile is interdependent. If you find yourself unable to complete a section, do not be afraid to clear the board and re-evaluate your initial assumptions regarding the most restrictive zones. Data from player feedback suggests that most errors occur when players overlook the 'Number' constraint for a specific zone, assuming that the entire domino must be inside the colored area when only half is required.
