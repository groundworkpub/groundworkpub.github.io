What you need to know before buying a car out of state

By Groundwork Editorial • 5 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Buying a car out of state requires careful planning. Learn how to navigate sales tax, registration, and emissions requirements to avoid costly mistakes.

In This Guide

  • How do you handle sales tax when buying out of state? Sales tax is determined by the state where you register the vehicle, not the state where you purchase it. Many buyers mistakenly believe they can save money by purchasing a car in a state with lower or no sales tax, but your home state’s Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) will collect the difference or the full tax amount when you apply for registration. If you purchase from a licensed dealership, they will often collect the tax on behalf of your home state as part of the transaction paperwork. If you buy from a private seller, you will be responsible for paying the full sales tax bill directly to your local DMV when you title the car. According to the Federal Trade Commission, you should always verify the tax reciprocity agreements between your state and the selling state to avoid double taxation or unexpected bills during the registration phase.
  • Why must you verify emissions and safety standards first? A vehicle that is roadworthy in one state may not meet the emissions or safety standards required for registration in your home state. States like California, for example, enforce strict Smog Check requirements that differ significantly from other jurisdictions, as noted by the California Department of Motor Vehicles. Before you finalize a purchase, you must check your state’s DMV website to see if the vehicle requires a specific VIN inspection, a safety inspection, or an emissions test. If the vehicle is older or modified, it may fail to meet the specific environmental standards of your state, effectively rendering it impossible to register. Failing to verify these requirements can result in owning a vehicle that you cannot legally drive on public roads in your home state.
  • What paperwork is required to transport the vehicle home? To legally drive a car from the point of purchase to your home, you must secure temporary transit permits or a temporary registration. These documents allow you to operate the vehicle on public roads without having permanent plates assigned yet. If you are buying from a dealership, they are typically experienced in handling these out-of-state transit permits and can often issue a temporary tag. If you are purchasing from a private party, you may need to visit the local DMV in the selling state to obtain a temporary moving permit. Additionally, you must contact your insurance provider before you take possession of the vehicle. Driving a new car without active coverage is a liability risk that no amount of savings on the purchase price can justify.
  • How to organize the registration process 1. Confirm the vehicle’s VIN and title status: Ensure the title is clear and that the VIN matches the physical plates on the vehicle. 2. Review your home state’s DMV requirements: Visit your state’s official motor vehicle website to download the specific forms needed for out-of-state vehicle registration. 3. Secure insurance: Provide your insurance agent with the vehicle identification number (VIN) and the date you plan to take possession to ensure active coverage from the moment of purchase. 4. Obtain a bill of sale: Ensure you have a notarized bill of sale or an itemized dealer invoice, as your local tax office will require this to calculate sales tax. 5. Complete required inspections: If your state requires a VIN verification or emissions test, schedule these appointments immediately upon returning, as many states have a strict 10-to-30-day window for registering a newly acquired vehicle. 6. Submit your application: Bring your title, bill of sale, proof of insurance, and inspection certificates to your local DMV or county tax office to receive your permanent plates.
  • What are the hidden costs of out-of-state purchases? The primary hidden costs are the time and travel expenses required to secure the vehicle and the potential for regulatory hurdles. If the vehicle requires a specialized inspection that it fails, you may be forced to pay for repairs or emissions systems upgrades before the state will grant you a title. Furthermore, if you are financing the car, ensure your lender is comfortable with an out-of-state purchase, as some banks have specific restrictions on the types of dealerships they will fund. Always account for the cost of travel, fuel, and lodging when calculating the 'all-in' price of an out-of-state vehicle, as these costs often negate the perceived savings of a lower purchase price. By preparing the necessary documentation and confirming compliance before you sign the contract, you can mitigate the risk of a costly administrative error.

Buying a car out of state is the process of purchasing a vehicle from a dealership or private seller located outside of your primary state of residence. While the transaction itself follows the laws of the state where the sale occurs, the subsequent registration, taxation, and compliance requirements are dictated by the rules of your home state. Failing to account for these differences can lead to unexpected fees, registration delays, or even an inability to legally drive your new vehicle.

According to USA.gov, motor vehicle services are managed at the state level, meaning there is no uniform national process for transferring a title or registering an out-of-state vehicle. Because regulations vary significantly, you must treat the purchase as a two-part process: the acquisition and the homecoming.
