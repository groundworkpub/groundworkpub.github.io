How to identify and fix a popping sound in your wall

A popping sound in a wall is an audible mechanical or acoustic disturbance originating from the structural components, utility lines, or electrical systems housed behind your drywall. While homeowners often associate these sounds with thermal expansion, they can also stem from resonant vibrations caused by external audio sources or loose electrical components.

According to building science experts at Green Building Advisor, homeowners frequently misattribute acoustic phenomena to thermal movement when the actual cause is often a harmonic vibration or a loose connection (Green Building Advisor, 2026). Identifying the true source requires a systematic process of elimination.

Is the sound caused by thermal expansion? Thermal expansion is the physical process where materials, such as wooden wall studs or metal ductwork, expand when heated and contract when cooled, causing audible "pops" or "cracks" as they rub against fasteners or framing members. This is common in regions with significant temperature swings, such as Houston, Texas, where exterior temperatures can exceed 95 degrees Fahrenheit (Green Building Advisor, 2026).

If your walls pop specifically during sunrise or sunset, thermal movement is a likely culprit. As the sun hits the exterior siding, the materials heat up and expand; as the sun sets, the materials cool and contract. If the framing was installed tightly without proper expansion gaps, or if nails have slightly loosened over time, the friction between these materials produces a sharp, popping sound. While usually harmless, these sounds indicate that your wall assembly is under stress during temperature transitions.

Is the sound caused by harmonic vibration? If you notice the popping sound only when a television soundbar or speaker system is active, the issue is likely harmonic vibration rather than a structural fault. Harmonic resonance occurs when sound waves from your speakers hit a frequency that matches the natural vibration frequency of an object inside the wall, such as a loose pipe, a metal duct, or a poorly secured piece of electrical conduit (Green Building Advisor, 2026).

To test for this, play a variety of audio content through your soundbar at different volume levels. If the popping coincides with specific bass frequencies, you are likely dealing with a resonance issue. The sound isn't actually a "pop" in the sense of a material breaking; rather, it is a vibration caused by acoustic energy transferring into the wall cavity and shaking a loose component against the framing.

Could the sound be related to electrical components? Electrical-related popping sounds are distinct because they may be accompanied by a faint buzzing or clicking. If you have cables running through wall plates—specifically "pass-through" style plates—the wires inside the wall may be vibrating against the back of the drywall or the wooden studs due to electromagnetic interference or physical contact with a speaker wire (Green Building Advisor, 2026).

If you suspect an electrical cause, follow these steps: 1. Disconnect the soundbar from the power source entirely to see if the noise persists. If the noise stops, the interaction is definitely audio-related. 2. Check your cable management. If you are using pass-through plates, pull the cables taut and then adjust them to ensure they are not resting directly against the hollow back of the drywall. 3. Inspect your electrical outlets. If the popping sounds like a "snap" or "crackle" and occurs regardless of audio output, contact a licensed electrician immediately. An electrical arc, which is a dangerous discharge of electricity, can create a sharp popping sound and is a significant fire hazard (National Fire Protection Association, 2023).

How to troubleshoot the noise effectively To determine the root cause of the noise, you must isolate the variables systematically. Do not assume the wall is structurally failing, as most popping sounds in residential construction are cosmetic or acoustic in nature.

1. **Document the timing:** Keep a log of when the sound occurs. Is it linked to the time of day (thermal) or the use of specific appliances (vibration/electrical)? 2. **Isolate audio sources:** If the sound happens during music or television playback, move the soundbar or subwoofer away from the wall. If the sound stops, the vibration was caused by the proximity of the speakers to the wall structure. 3. **Test the framing:** Gently push on the drywall near the area where the sound originates. If the drywall feels "soft" or moves independently of the studs, you may have a loose fastener, which can be fixed by adding a new drywall screw into the stud. 4. **Consult a professional:** If you hear popping sounds accompanied by the smell of ozone or burning plastic, or if the sound occurs randomly without any external trigger, stop using the affected electrical circuit and call a professional to inspect the wiring behind the wall (NFPA, 2023).

By following these steps, you can distinguish between harmless thermal movement and potential electrical issues. If the noise is strictly related to soundbar output, it is almost certainly a vibration that can be mitigated by adjusting the placement of your audio equipment.
