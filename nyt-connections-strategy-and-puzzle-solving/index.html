How to solve NYT Connections puzzles like a pro

By Alex Rivera • 3 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Learn the expert strategies for solving NYT Connections, including how to spot red herrings, identify category themes, and use lateral thinking to win daily.

Outline

  • How to identify category themes To identify category themes, look for associations that span across different parts of speech or cultural domains. Start by examining the words for common prefixes, suffixes, or shared phonetic structures. If you find a potential category, such as "Black-and-yellow" items (e.g., bumblebee, caution tape, Charlie Brown, Wolverine), test whether the remaining words can form three other cohesive groups. If you have five words that seem to fit one category, you are likely dealing with a distractor designed to lead you away from the correct solution.
  • Why do the puzzle editors use red herrings? Editors use red herrings to increase difficulty by creating "obvious" groups that are technically incorrect. For example, a puzzle might include four words related to comic books, but only three of those words belong to the category "Comic book artist's tools" (such as Bristol board, dip pen, and India ink). The fourth word is likely a "trap" word that belongs in a different category entirely. You can mitigate this by shuffling the board frequently; changing the visual layout of the words often helps the brain break out of rigid patterns and notice new, non-obvious relationships.
  • Effective strategies for solving Connections 1. Say the words aloud: Verbalizing the list helps you hear rhythmic patterns or common phrases that aren't apparent on the screen. Editors frequently use "fill-in-the-blank" style categories, such as words that follow or precede a specific term. 2. Break down compound words: If a word seems out of place, analyze its components. A word like "Rushmore" might not fit a category based on geography, but its prefix might relate to a band name or a specific historical reference. 3. Analyze potential categories before submitting: Before locking in a group, ensure that the remaining 12 words form three valid categories of four. Guessing blindly often wastes attempts, as the game limits the number of mistakes you can make. 4. Prioritize the most restrictive groups: If you identify a very niche category—such as "Triangle logos" (e.g., Bass Ale, Citgo, Delta Airlines, Google Drive)—tackle that first. Highly specific groups are often easier to isolate than broad, thematic ones like "places where everyone thinks the same way" (e.g., bubble, echo chamber, hive mind, silo).
  • Managing your mental approach Avoid "tunnel vision" by taking a step back when you feel stuck. If you have been staring at the same four words for several minutes without a breakthrough, walk away for a moment. Cognitive psychology research suggests that "incubation periods" allow the subconscious to process information, often leading to a sudden realization upon returning to the task. When you return, treat the puzzle as a fresh set of data points rather than a continuation of your previous frustration.

NYT Connections is a daily word-categorization game where players must organize 16 words into four distinct groups of four based on shared, often subtle, characteristics. Mastering this game requires moving beyond literal interpretations of words to identify thematic, linguistic, or cultural connections that the puzzle editors intentionally obscure.

According to data from The New York Times, the game challenges players to recognize patterns under tight constraints, often using "red herrings"—words that seem to fit multiple categories but only belong to one specific group. Successfully solving the puzzle requires a blend of pattern recognition, lateral thinking, and vocabulary depth.
