👉 Read the Full Study on Groundwork: Why the J.H. Campbell power plant remains operational

An emergency order is a federal directive issued by the U.S. Secretary of Energy to force power plants to remain operational beyond their planned retirement dates to ensure grid stability. In the case of the J.H. Campbell coal-fired plant in West Olive, Michigan, this order prevents a 1,420-megawatt facility from shutting down, maintaining its availability to meet electricity demand in the Midwest region.

Grid reliability, or the ability of the electrical system to deliver consistent power during peak demand, has become a primary focus for the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE). According to the DOE, emergency orders are utilized when regional grid operators, such as the Midwest Independent System Operator (MISO), face potential shortfalls where projected energy supply cannot keep pace with increasing electricity consumption (Department of Energy, 2026).

Why is the J.H. Campbell plant being kept open?

The J.H. Campbell plant is being kept operational because it acts as a critical "baseload" power source during periods when renewable energy production, such as wind and solar, is insufficient to meet regional demand. The U.S. Secretary of Energy issued an emergency order directing MISO and Consumers Energy to keep the 1,420-megawatt facility online to prevent potential grid failures or load shedding in the Midwest (Department of Energy, 2026).

Originally, the Campbell plant was scheduled for decommissioning on May 31, 2025, nearly 15 years before the end of its intended mechanical lifespan. However, repeated emergency orders—dating back to May 2025—have compelled the facility to continue generating electricity. The current order, effective from August 17, 2026, through November 14, 2026, ensures that the plant remains available to dispatch power whenever the grid requires additional stability.

What does the NERC report say about Midwest grid reliability?

The North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) has identified the MISO region as having a high risk of energy shortfalls over the next five years due to a mismatch between rising demand and the retirement of existing power plants. In its 2025 Long-Term Reliability Assessment, NERC warned that resource additions are failing to keep pace with the energy needs of the region (NERC, 2026).

Furthermore, the 2026 State of Reliability report highlighted that "shoulder seasons"—the periods between peak winter and summer demand—are becoming increasingly difficult for grid operators to manage. NERC noted that these challenges have already resulted in instances of "pre-contingent load shedding," which is a controlled reduction of power to prevent broader, uncontrolled blackouts across the grid.

How do emergency orders affect electricity costs?

Emergency orders like the one applied to the Campbell plant are intended to minimize costs for consumers by utilizing "economic dispatch" protocols. Economic dispatch is a process where grid operators select the most cost-effective combination of available power plants to meet real-time electricity demand. By keeping the Campbell plant available, MISO can draw power from an existing, operational facility rather than relying on more expensive or less reliable market alternatives during supply crunches.

While the primary goal of these orders is to maintain grid reliability, the DOE emphasizes that maintaining existing infrastructure is often more cost-effective than the immediate construction of new transmission lines or generation facilities. For the average household, this policy is designed to mitigate the price spikes that occur when energy supply is extremely limited relative to the total load on the grid.

What are the long-term risks to grid stability?

The long-term risk to the electrical grid is a phenomenon known as resource adequacy, which refers to having enough power plants available to meet demand at all times. The U.S. Department of Energy has warned that if the retirement of "dispatchable" power plants—facilities that can run regardless of weather conditions—continues at a pace faster than new, equally reliable resources can be built, the risk of blackouts increases significantly (Department of Energy, 2026).

To manage these risks, regulators are currently evaluating the balance between transitioning to low-carbon energy sources and maintaining a baseline of reliable power. The current strategy involves using intermittent emergency orders to "bridge" the gap until new grid infrastructure, such as battery storage or modernized transmission, can be deployed at scale. Until then, plants like J.H. Campbell serve as essential buffers against grid instability.

What should you do if you are concerned about grid reliability?

If you are worried about the reliability of your local power grid, you should first check with your local utility provider to see if they participate in a regional transmission organization (RTO) like MISO. Most utility companies provide public access to their resource planning documents, which outline their projected energy needs and plans for grid maintenance over the coming decade.

For homeowners, investing in energy efficiency measures can reduce the strain on the grid during peak times. Steps include:

1. Install a programmable thermostat to reduce HVAC usage during high-demand hours. 2. Upgrade to ENERGY STAR-certified appliances to lower your total electricity consumption. 3. Consider a home backup power solution, such as a battery energy storage system or a standby generator, if you live in an area prone to frequent service interruptions. 4. Participate in "demand response" programs offered by your utility, which may offer rebates or lower rates in exchange for reducing your power usage during grid emergencies.
