What the Relay shutdown means for the future of browser-based AI

By Groundwork Editorial • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

The shutdown of AI startup Relay and its integration into Google Chrome signals a shift toward browser-based AI agents. Here is what it means for your workflow.

In This Guide

  • Why startups are choosing integration over independence
  • How Google is reshaping the browser as an operating system
  • What happens to your data when a tool shuts down?
  • The shift toward 'agentic' workflows
  • How to prepare for the future of browser-based automation

An 'acqui-hire' is a business strategy where a large corporation acquires a smaller startup primarily to gain access to its talent and intellectual property rather than its existing product or revenue stream. The recent closure of Relay, an AI-powered workflow automation platform, and the subsequent transition of its core team to Google’s Chrome division represents a significant shift in how tech giants are prioritizing browser-based AI integration. At Groundwork, our analysis indicates that this move signals a pivot from standalone productivity tools toward embedding AI agents directly into the browser environment.

Why startups are choosing integration over independence

Standalone AI startups often face a 'distribution wall' where the cost of acquiring users exceeds the lifetime value of those users. Relay, which launched in 2021 with the ambition of becoming a robust alternative to automation platforms like Zapier, ultimately concluded that its technology could have a greater reach within a native browser environment. By joining forces with Google, the Relay team gains immediate access to Chrome’s user base, which accounts for over 65% of global browser market share according to StatCounter. For the average user, this means that independent, specialized productivity tools are increasingly likely to be absorbed into larger ecosystems, effectively ending their existence as separate applications.

How Google is reshaping the browser as an operating system

The acquisition of Relay’s talent is part of a broader trend of Google treating the Chrome browser as a primary interface for AI interaction. Historically, a browser was a passive portal for viewing web pages; today, it is becoming an active agent-based environment. Google’s integration of Gemini into Chrome—which now serves as an optional browser-based assistant—complements this strategy. By bringing in leadership from Relay, Google is signaling that the next phase of its AI roadmap involves turning the browser into a collaborative space for agents. Our research suggests that the browser is becoming the 'operating system for the internet,' where automation tasks like drafting documents, managing project workflows, and handling repetitive data entry occur without the need to switch between dozens of individual browser tabs.

What happens to your data when a tool shuts down?

The closure of Relay underscores the risks associated with relying on venture-backed productivity tools. When Relay ceased operations, paying customers were given a strict deadline to export their data before access was terminated on September 14, 2026. This highlights the importance of data portability when choosing software. Before investing time in an AI-driven workflow tool, you should assess two critical factors: the startup’s funding runway and the ease of exporting your automated workflows to a standard format (such as JSON or CSV). If a tool does not allow for a clean exit, you are essentially locked into a service that could disappear with little warning, forcing you to rebuild your automation logic from scratch.

The shift toward 'agentic' workflows

Relay’s original value proposition centered on workflow automation, a concept now evolving into 'agentic AI.' Unlike simple macros or basic automation, agentic AI refers to systems capable of reasoning through multi-step tasks independently. Jacob Bank, the former CEO of Relay and now VP of Product for Chrome, has explicitly framed the browser as a 'perfect place to collaborate with agents.' This indicates that the future of Chrome will likely feature AI that does not just answer questions (like a chatbot) but performs complex tasks across different web applications on your behalf. For the consumer, this means the browser will soon handle tasks such as comparing prices, filing expense reports, or scheduling meetings by interacting directly with the sites you visit.

How to prepare for the future of browser-based automation

If you are currently using niche AI tools for productivity, you should prepare for a landscape where these features become native to your browser. To protect your workflow, follow these steps:

1. Audit your dependencies: Identify which workflows rely on third-party AI extensions that lack a clear long-term business model. 2. Prioritize interoperability: Choose tools that utilize open APIs (Application Programming Interfaces), which makes it easier to migrate your data if the service provider shuts down. 3. Monitor platform native features: As Chrome and other browsers integrate more AI capabilities, test these native features to see if they can replace your paid third-party tools, thereby reducing your 'tool sprawl.' 4. Maintain local backups: Always keep a record of the logic or 'recipes' you have built in your automation tools, as these are often more valuable than the actual data processed.

By focusing on these core principles, you can mitigate the risks associated with the rapid consolidation of AI startups. At Groundwork, we emphasize that while the loss of an independent tool is inconvenient, the integration of that technology into a stable, massive ecosystem like Chrome often leads to more robust and long-term utility for the end user.
