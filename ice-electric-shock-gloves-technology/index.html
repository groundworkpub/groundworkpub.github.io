The technology behind ICE's proposed electric shock gloves

An electric shock glove is a wearable restraint device designed to deliver a high-voltage, low-amperage electrical current through the wearer’s fingertips to incapacitate or deter a subject upon physical contact. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) has issued a procurement notice for the CTG 5 GLOVE, manufactured by Compliant Technologies, as part of a potential $20 million contract to equip agents with non-lethal de-escalation tools by March 2027 (The Verge, 2024). This technology represents a shift toward contact-based compliance tools in federal law enforcement operations.

How the CTG 5 glove functions

The CTG 5 GLOVE, marketed by the manufacturer as a Conductive Distraction and De-escalation (CD3) device, operates by delivering a localized electric pulse. Unlike a traditional Taser, which utilizes projectile probes to complete a circuit, these gloves require the agent to make direct physical contact with the subject to trigger the shock. According to the manufacturer, the device is intended to provide a "low-optics" solution, meaning it is designed to look like standard-issue protective gear, thereby minimizing the visual spectacle often associated with traditional batons or firearms during an altercation (Compliant Technologies, 2024).

The technical specifications and output

While the specific voltage and amperage settings for the ICE contract remain subject to final procurement terms, the device is categorized as a Generated Low Output Voltage Emitter. These systems typically rely on internal battery packs integrated into the glove's wrist or forearm cuff. The electrical output is designed to cause involuntary muscle contractions or sensory overload, theoretically distracting the subject long enough for an officer to gain physical control. The primary technical challenge for this hardware is ensuring that the electrical pulse is sufficient to deter aggression without causing permanent tissue damage or cardiac events, a threshold that remains a subject of intense debate among human rights organizations and medical professionals (Amnesty International, 2023).

Rationale for non-lethal force integration

ICE officials have framed the adoption of these devices as a move toward "humane" de-escalation. By providing agents with a tool that allows for immediate physical compliance without the use of lethal force, the agency aims to reduce the likelihood of severe injury during immigration enforcement actions. Data from the Department of Homeland Security suggests that federal agencies are increasingly prioritizing "less-than-lethal" technology to mitigate liability and reduce the public visibility of force incidents. However, critics argue that the proximity required to use the gloves increases the risk of physical altercations, potentially putting agents in more dangerous positions than they would be in while using distance-based tools like pepper spray or projectile devices.

Ethical and operational considerations

Implementing shock-delivery hardware in a custodial or enforcement setting introduces significant ethical concerns regarding the potential for misuse. Because the device is concealed within a glove, the threshold for activating an electric shock may be lower than that for drawing a weapon, leading to concerns about "mission creep," where non-lethal tools are used to encourage compliance in non-violent scenarios. Furthermore, the reliance on proprietary technology from a single vendor raises questions about long-term maintenance, training requirements, and the independent verification of safety standards. Before these devices are deployed, standard operating procedures must be established to track the frequency and justification of each activation, ensuring that the use of force remains documented and auditable.
