Why cancer surgery wait times are increasing in the US

👉 Read the Full Study on Groundwork: Why cancer surgery wait times are increasing in the US

Cancer surgery wait time is the period between a patient’s initial diagnosis and the date they receive their first definitive surgical procedure. Recent research indicates that these intervals have trended upward across the United States over the last decade, potentially impacting patient survival rates and long-term health outcomes.

A study published in JAMA Surgery analyzed data from over 2.7 million patients diagnosed with stage I–III non-metastatic cancers, including breast, colon, lung, pancreatic, gastric, and esophageal cancers. Between 2012 and 2023, the researchers found that patients consistently faced longer delays before reaching the operating room, a trend that persists even as surgical techniques and oncology treatments have become more advanced.

How are wait times for cancer surgery measured?

Wait times for cancer surgery are measured by calculating the number of days elapsed from the date of a confirmed diagnosis to the date of the primary surgical intervention. This window is a critical metric because timely access to care is strongly associated with better clinical outcomes, reduced tumor progression, and lower levels of patient psychological distress.

The JAMA Surgery study examined data across four distinct time periods: 2012–2015, 2016–2019, 2020–2021, and 2022–2023. By tracking these cohorts, researchers identified a persistent increase in the time patients wait for surgery. While surgical outcomes for cancer have generally improved due to technological advancements, the systemic delay in getting patients to the operating table creates a bottleneck that may undermine these medical gains.

Why are surgical delays becoming more common?

Surgical delays are becoming more common due to a combination of hospital capacity constraints, staffing shortages, and the increasing complexity of oncology care pathways. As diagnostic technology improves, health systems are managing higher volumes of patients, which can stretch administrative and surgical resources thin, leading to longer scheduling queues.

Furthermore, the integration of multidisciplinary care teams—where oncologists, surgeons, radiologists, and pathologists must coordinate—often adds layers of logistical complexity. While this team-based approach is intended to provide better care, it requires significant synchronization that can delay the final surgical date if communication or scheduling workflows are not optimized. The COVID-19 pandemic also exacerbated these issues, creating a massive backlog of non-urgent and elective procedures that disrupted typical cancer care timelines across the US.

What are the clinical risks of waiting for surgery?

Waiting for surgery increases the risk of tumor progression, which can shift a patient from an early, curable stage to a more advanced, difficult-to-treat stage. Clinical evidence suggests that even minor delays can negatively impact survival rates, particularly in aggressive cancers like pancreatic or esophageal, where time is a critical factor in stopping the spread of disease.

Beyond physical risks, prolonged wait times contribute to significant patient anxiety and psychological distress. The "scanxiety" and uncertainty that accompany a diagnosis are amplified when a patient knows they need a procedure but cannot secure a date. This period of waiting can diminish a patient's overall quality of life and may lead to a loss of trust in the healthcare system.

How can patients advocate for timely care?

To ensure timely care, you should maintain open communication with your oncology team and request a clear timeline for your treatment plan immediately following a diagnosis. If your surgery is delayed, ask your provider for a specific explanation and inquire whether the delay poses a clinical risk to your prognosis.

1. **Request a timeline:** Ask your surgeon or navigator for the target date for your procedure as soon as a treatment plan is finalized. 2. **Inquire about cancellations:** Ask if you can be placed on a priority list to fill any last-minute cancellations in the operating room schedule. 3. **Seek a second opinion:** If a facility cannot provide a surgery date within an acceptable timeframe, consult with another major cancer center or academic hospital to see if they have better availability. 4. **Utilize patient navigators:** Many hospitals employ nurse navigators whose specific job is to help patients move through the system and overcome administrative hurdles.
