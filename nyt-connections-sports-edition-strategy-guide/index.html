How to master nyt connections: sports edition

By Groundwork Editorial • 3 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Learn how to master NYT Connections: Sports Edition with expert strategies for identifying categories, managing mistakes, and solving the daily grid.

Connections: Sports Edition is a daily word-categorization game developed by The New York Times in partnership with The Athletic. The game requires players to organize a grid of 16 words into four distinct groups of four, where each group shares a specific thematic connection. Unlike the original Connections, this version focuses exclusively on sports-related terminology, athletes, teams, and historical events.

How to play connections: sports edition

To play Connections: Sports Edition, you must identify thematic links between 16 provided words. You select four words at a time and submit them as a group. If your grouping is correct, those words are removed from the board and categorized by a specific color code: yellow for the easiest, followed by green, blue, and purple for the most challenging. You are permitted a maximum of four mistakes before the game ends, according to the official game rules provided by The New York Times (NYT, 2026).

What are the best strategies for solving the grid?

Successful play in Connections relies on identifying "distractor" words that seem to belong to multiple categories. To improve your performance, start by scanning all 16 words for obvious clusters. If you see terms like "Chiefs" or "Eagles," your first instinct may be to group them as NFL teams. However, the game designers often include words that fit two potential categories to force you into making a mistake. Always pause to check if a word could belong to a different group before locking in your selection.

How do the difficulty levels work?

Difficulty in Connections is represented by color-coded categories. Yellow groups are generally straightforward, often involving direct synonyms or well-known sports entities. Green categories typically involve slightly more obscure associations, such as regional sports teams (e.g., Tampa Bay-area franchises). Blue categories often involve wordplay or fill-in-the-blank structures, such as "Matt___," where you must identify a common prefix or suffix. Purple categories are consistently the most difficult, frequently requiring lateral thinking, such as identifying NBA teams where the first letter has been altered to create a new word.

Why does the game use categories like "nba teams with the first letter changed"?

This specific type of category is a staple of advanced Connections puzzles because it forces you to look beyond the literal meaning of the words. When you encounter a list of words that look like non-sports terms but possess a rhythmic or structural similarity to team names, test them against common league rosters. By removing the first letter of each word and replacing it with another, you may find the underlying team name. This technique, corroborated by common word-game design patterns, is designed to test both your sports knowledge and your linguistic flexibility.

How to manage your four mistakes

Because you only have four attempts to solve the puzzle, managing your "lives" is critical. If you are unsure about a specific grouping, use the shuffle button to rearrange the grid. A fresh visual perspective often helps reveal connections that were obscured by the initial layout. If you remain stuck, prioritize clearing the yellow and green categories first to reduce the number of variables on the board, making the harder blue and purple categories easier to deduce by elimination.

Practical steps for daily improvement

To improve your consistency, follow this process each day: 1. Scan the full board for obvious, high-confidence matches. 2. Identify words that appear in two potential categories and hold off on grouping them until you are certain of the other three members of the set. 3. Use the shuffle feature frequently to break your mental fixation on specific word positions. 4. If you have two potential groups of four but are unsure, try to solve the most obvious one first to shrink the pool of remaining options. 5. When stuck, look for wordplay patterns like prefixes, suffixes, or anagrams rather than just topical themes like "Super Bowl winners."
