Why tertiary cities are the smartest choice for your next getaway

By Groundwork Editorial • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Discover why tertiary cities offer a better balance of culture and affordability. Learn how to plan a high-quality getaway without the major city price tag.

In This Guide

  • The economic advantage of smaller markets
  • Cultural density and accessibility
  • Prioritizing the "slow travel" experience
  • Strategic planning for your trip
  • The long-term value of regional exploration

You do not need to visit a major metropolitan hub to find a high-quality travel experience. In fact, tertiary cities—metropolitan areas with populations under 500,000—often provide a superior balance of cultural depth, localized charm, and fiscal accessibility compared to their primary city counterparts. By choosing these smaller destinations, you avoid the inflated costs associated with major tourist centers while supporting regional economies and enjoying a more authentic, less congested atmosphere.

Recent data from the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis indicates that the cost of living and leisure spending in smaller metropolitan statistical areas remains significantly lower than in top-tier cities, allowing your travel budget to extend further without sacrificing quality or comfort.

The economic advantage of smaller markets

When you travel to a primary city like New York or San Francisco, a substantial portion of your budget is absorbed by high-demand lodging and premium dining markups. In contrast, tertiary cities often operate on a different economic scale. Because these areas are not primarily driven by high-volume tourism, local businesses prioritize quality and community reputation over high-turnover pricing. You will frequently find that boutique hotels, independent restaurants, and unique cultural experiences are priced at a level that reflects the local cost of living rather than global market demand.

Furthermore, these cities often benefit from lower "tourist taxes" and fewer hidden fees. When you choose a tertiary destination, you are often paying for the actual value of the service provided rather than the premium of being in a high-traffic urban center.

Cultural density and accessibility

There is a common misconception that smaller populations correlate with a lack of cultural sophistication. However, many tertiary cities act as regional hubs for the arts, culinary innovation, and outdoor recreation. Bentonville, Arkansas, serves as a prime example. Despite having a population well under 100,000, it houses the Crystal Bridges Museum of American Art, a world-class institution that offers free admission. This accessibility is rarely found in larger cities where museum entry fees can reach $30 or more per person.

These cities often punch above their weight because they are centers for regional talent. Chefs, artists, and entrepreneurs who might be priced out of larger markets often gravitate toward these smaller, growing cities, bringing their expertise and creativity with them. This results in a "hyper-local" experience where the food you eat and the art you view are deeply connected to the geography and history of the location.

Prioritizing the "slow travel" experience

Tertiary cities are inherently designed for a slower pace of life. Places like San Luis Obispo, California, embody this philosophy. Instead of racing between landmarks, you are encouraged to engage with the environment through walkable downtowns, regional farmers markets, and outdoor activities. This approach reduces the stress of logistics and increases the quality of your leisure time. When you are not spending three hours a day commuting across a sprawling metropolis, you have more time to patronize local businesses and interact with the community.

Strategic planning for your trip

To maximize the benefits of a tertiary city getaway, follow these steps:

1. Research regional hubs: Look for cities that serve as the economic center for a specific geographic region (e.g., the Ozarks or the Central Coast of California). These areas usually have the best infrastructure for visitors. 2. Leverage loyalty programs: Even smaller cities have presence from major hotel chains like Hilton, Accor, or Marriott. You can often use points in these locations at a better redemption rate than in major cities where demand is perpetually high. 3. Prioritize local transit and walkability: Choose a hotel or rental that allows you to walk or bike to the main attractions. This eliminates the need for a rental car, which is often the most expensive component of a domestic trip. 4. Engage with local tourism boards: Smaller cities rely on local tourism boards to curate experiences. Visit their official websites before you arrive to find free events, walking tours, and seasonal festivals that might not be listed on major travel aggregators.

The long-term value of regional exploration

By diversifying your travel habits to include tertiary cities, you are not just saving money; you are investing in a more sustainable model of tourism. Smaller cities are often more resilient to the negative impacts of over-tourism and benefit directly from your visit. Whether you are looking for outdoor adventure, culinary discovery, or a quiet space to reset, these smaller metropolitan areas provide the infrastructure you need at a price point that makes frequent travel more achievable. Start by identifying one region you have ignored in favor of a major city and research its local cultural calendar; you will likely find that the experience is just as fulfilling, if not more so, than a trip to a traditional tourist hotspot.
