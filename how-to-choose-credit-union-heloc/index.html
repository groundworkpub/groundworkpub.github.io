How to choose a credit union for a home equity line of credit

By Groundwork Editorial • 5 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Learn how to evaluate credit unions for a home equity line of credit (HELOC). Compare rates, fees, and membership requirements to find the best loan terms.

  • Comparing interest rates and fee structures
  • Evaluating eligibility and membership requirements
  • Assessing customer service and technology
  • The importance of loan-to-value (LTV) ratios
  • Next steps for your application

A home equity line of credit (HELOC) allows you to borrow against the equity in your property, providing a flexible source of funds for home improvements, debt consolidation, or emergency expenses. While major national banks often dominate the lending market, credit unions frequently offer more competitive interest rates and personalized service for homeowners. Choosing the right institution requires evaluating fee structures, borrowing limits, and the specific terms of the draw and repayment periods.

According to data from the National Credit Union Administration (NCUA), credit unions often provide lower average interest rates on consumer loans compared to commercial banks, primarily due to their not-for-profit cooperative structure.

Understanding the HELOC structure

A HELOC functions like a revolving credit card secured by your home. During the 'draw period'—typically lasting five to ten years—you can borrow funds up to a predetermined limit, pay down the balance, and borrow again as needed. Most lenders require interest-only payments during this time, though you can choose to pay down the principal to reduce total interest costs. Once the draw period concludes, you enter the 'repayment period,' where you are no longer allowed to borrow, and you must pay back the remaining balance in full, including both principal and interest, over a set term, usually 10 to 20 years.

When evaluating a credit union, ask specifically how they handle the transition between these two phases. Some institutions offer the option to convert a variable-rate HELOC into a fixed-rate loan during the repayment period, which can protect you from rising interest rates. Understanding this flexibility is critical for long-term financial planning.

Comparing interest rates and fee structures

Credit unions are member-owned, which often leads to lower overhead costs and fewer "junk fees" compared to large retail banks. However, you must look beyond the advertised Annual Percentage Rate (APR). While the APR provides a standardized way to compare costs, it may not account for all upfront charges.

Common fees to watch for include: - **Origination fees:** A percentage of the total credit line, often ranging from 0.5% to 1%. - **Appraisal fees:** The cost to verify the current market value of your home. - **Annual fees:** Some credit unions charge a yearly maintenance fee, regardless of whether you have an active balance. - **Early termination fees:** Penalties for closing the account within the first few years of the draw period.

Always request a Loan Estimate (LE) from any credit union you are considering. This document is standardized by federal law and allows you to compare the "bottom line" costs side-by-side between different lenders.

Evaluating eligibility and membership requirements

Unlike traditional banks, credit unions require membership to access lending products. Membership is typically based on a "field of membership," which may be defined by your geographic location, your employer, or participation in a specific organization.

Before applying for a HELOC, confirm your eligibility status. If you do not meet the primary criteria, look for local community-based credit unions or those with broad, inclusive membership requirements. Many credit unions allow you to qualify simply by making a small donation to a partner non-profit organization, which can open the door to their financial products.

Assessing customer service and technology

While credit unions are known for personal service, their digital capabilities vary significantly. As you evaluate potential lenders, consider how you prefer to manage your finances. If you rely on mobile banking for transfers and payment tracking, ensure the credit union provides a robust app and an intuitive online portal.

Furthermore, consider the accessibility of loan officers. One of the primary advantages of a credit union is the ability to speak with a human who understands your local market. During the underwriting process, this can be a significant benefit if your financial situation is slightly outside the standard "cookie-cutter" profile required by large, automated banking algorithms.

The importance of loan-to-value (LTV) ratios

Every lender has a maximum loan-to-value (LTV) ratio, which determines how much equity you are permitted to borrow against. For example, if your home is worth $400,000 and you have a $200,000 mortgage, your current equity is $200,000. If a credit union has a maximum LTV of 80%, they will allow a total debt (mortgage plus HELOC) of $320,000. This means you could access up to $120,000 in credit.

If you have significant equity, some credit unions may offer more favorable LTV ratios than others. If you are close to the threshold, it is worth checking with multiple credit unions to see which is willing to offer the highest borrowing power based on your specific property appraisal.

Next steps for your application

To begin the process, organize your financial records. Lenders will require recent pay stubs, W-2s, tax returns from the last two years, and your current mortgage statement. Start by checking your credit score, as this remains the primary factor in determining the interest rate you are offered. Once you have your documents prepared, reach out to at least three different credit unions to request a quote. Use the Loan Estimate document to compare the total costs and terms, and don't hesitate to ask about waiving application or appraisal fees. By comparing multiple offers, you ensure that you are securing the most favorable terms for your specific financial situation.
