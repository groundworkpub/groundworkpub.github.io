A legal bond is a financial guarantee required by a court to protect a party from potential damages caused by a temporary injunction or legal delay. In the high-stakes $110 billion merger between Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery (WBD), Paramount is petitioning a judge to force the state attorneys general (AGs) challenging the deal to post a $1.88 billion bond to cover the mounting costs of litigation-induced delays.

At Groundwork, our analysis shows that this request represents a rare application of the Clayton Antitrust Act, which allows defendants to seek financial security when a plaintiff attempts to block a transaction through litigation. If the court grants this motion, the states involved would effectively be on the hook for the financial erosion caused by the extended timeline.

Why is Paramount requesting a $1.88 billion bond? Paramount is requesting a $1.88 billion bond because the ongoing antitrust lawsuit has forced a delay in the deal's closing, triggering expensive contractual obligations known as 'ticking fees.' These fees represent the financial penalty for failing to close by the original target date of September 30, 2026. Because the merger involves a massive exchange of assets and shareholder value, the cost of waiting is not merely theoretical; it is a contractually mandated cash outflow.

According to the merger agreement, Paramount is obligated to pay WBD shareholders an additional 25 cents per share, per quarter, for every quarter the deal remains unclosed past the original deadline. With these payments estimated at roughly $650 million in cash value per quarter, the total cost of delay is compounding rapidly. By seeking a bond, Paramount is attempting to shift the economic burden of this delay onto the state AGs, arguing that if the states fail to prove their antitrust case, they should be liable for the damages their intervention caused the merging entities.

What does the Clayton Antitrust Act say about merger bonds? The Clayton Antitrust Act is a foundational piece of U.S. law that prohibits mergers and acquisitions that significantly lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly. While the Act is primarily used by regulators to block deals, it also contains provisions regarding the rights of companies to seek recourse when they are harmed by improper injunctions or litigation tactics. Paramount’s legal strategy relies on the principle that plaintiffs—in this case, the state AGs—should provide security for potential harm if the court eventually finds the merger was, in fact, lawful.

Historically, courts are hesitant to require government entities to post bonds. However, in cases involving massive commercial transactions where the 'harm' is quantifiable and immediate, the precedent shifts toward protecting the companies from avoidable financial distress. At Groundwork, our research into antitrust litigation suggests that while the request is aggressive, it is a logical move to force a resolution or, at the very least, ensure the states have 'skin in the game' regarding the financial impact of their legal challenge.

How do 'ticking fees' impact merger valuations? 'Ticking fees' are essentially a time-value-of-money penalty designed to compensate the target company’s shareholders for the uncertainty and delay caused by regulatory hurdles. In the Paramount-WBD context, these fees are not merely a nuisance; they are a significant component of the total deal valuation. As the clock ticks past September 30, the net cost of the acquisition increases for the buyer, which can potentially lead to a renegotiation of terms or, in extreme cases, the total abandonment of the deal if the costs become prohibitive.

For investors, the ticking fee serves as an indicator of the deal’s health. A rising fee suggests that the closing date is drifting further into the future, which lowers the internal rate of return (IRR) for the acquiring entity. Because Paramount has already secured approval from the U.S. Department of Justice and other international regulators, the ticking fee is specifically tied to the state-level legal holdup, which is the primary obstacle to the merger's finalization.

What are the potential outcomes for the merger? There are three primary paths forward for this merger as the trial date approaches in March 2027. First, the court could grant the bond request, which would place immense pressure on the state AGs to either expedite their case or demonstrate that the risk of harm to the public outweighs the financial damage to the corporations. Second, the court could deny the request, leaving Paramount to absorb the costs of the delay, which could potentially lead them to seek a lower purchase price from WBD to offset the losses.

Finally, the parties could move toward a settlement. In many large-scale antitrust cases, a settlement involves the divestiture of specific assets—such as certain pay TV networks or streaming assets—that the states claim would create a monopoly. By selling off these assets, the companies could satisfy the states' concerns and resolve the lawsuit without waiting for a full trial, effectively stopping the ticking fees before they reach the $1.88 billion threshold.
