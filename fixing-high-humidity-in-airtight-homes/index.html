How to fix high humidity in airtight homes

By Groundwork Editorial • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Airtight homes trap moisture, leading to high humidity. Learn why your mini-splits aren't enough and how to use ventilation or dehumidifiers to fix it.

High indoor humidity in an airtight home occurs because modern, high-performance building envelopes trap moisture generated by occupants and daily activities without providing a path for that moisture to escape. When a home is sealed with spray foam and new windows, it effectively becomes a closed system that requires mechanical intervention to manage moisture levels effectively.

Why are my mini-splits failing to control humidity? Mini-split heat pumps are designed primarily to manage temperature, not latent moisture, which is why they often struggle in tightly sealed environments. Because these systems are highly efficient, they reach the target thermostat temperature quickly and cycle off before the evaporator coil can run long enough to condense and remove significant amounts of moisture from the air.

When a home is extremely airtight, the internal moisture load—from cooking, showering, and breathing—accumulates rapidly. Since the mini-split satisfies the temperature setpoint in just a few minutes, the cooling cycle is too short to perform its secondary function of dehumidification. According to building science experts at Green Building Advisor, this is a common "short-cycling" issue that leaves occupants with cool, damp, and uncomfortable indoor air.

How does ventilation impact indoor humidity levels? Mechanical ventilation is the process of exchanging stale, moisture-laden indoor air with fresh outdoor air to maintain healthy indoor air quality and regulate humidity. In an airtight home, you cannot rely on "leaky" building materials for air exchange; you must install a system that controls the volume and source of the air entering your living space.

There are three primary types of mechanical ventilation systems used to manage these conditions: 1. **Exhaust-only ventilation:** These systems use fans to pull air out of the house, relying on passive vents to pull fresh air in. While simple, they can struggle to pull in enough air to manage humidity in very tight homes. 2. **Supply-only ventilation:** These systems push filtered outdoor air into the home, creating positive pressure. This can be effective, but in humid climates, it may introduce excess moisture if the incoming air is not conditioned. 3. **Balanced ventilation (HRV/ERV):** Heat Recovery Ventilators (HRV) and Energy Recovery Ventilators (ERV) are the gold standard for airtight homes. An ERV is specifically designed to transfer moisture between the incoming and outgoing air streams, which helps moderate humidity levels throughout the year.

Should I install a dedicated dehumidifier? If mechanical ventilation alone cannot keep indoor relative humidity between 30% and 50%, a whole-house dehumidifier is the most reliable, permanent solution. Unlike a mini-split, a dedicated dehumidifier is designed specifically to remove moisture without significantly cooling the air, allowing it to run independently of your heating and cooling system.

Standalone dehumidifiers can be integrated directly into your home’s ductwork or installed as a bypass unit. By setting the unit to a specific relative humidity target, you ensure that the air remains comfortable regardless of whether the mini-split is running. This approach is backed by research from the Department of Energy, which notes that controlling moisture is essential for preventing mold growth and protecting the structural integrity of a high-performance home.

What is the process for diagnosing and fixing the issue? To permanently resolve high humidity, you must follow a systematic approach that addresses both air exchange and moisture removal. Follow these steps to restore comfort:

1. **Measure baseline humidity:** Purchase a reliable digital hygrometer to confirm that your humidity levels are consistently above 60%. 2. **Evaluate your current ventilation:** If you do not have a mechanical ventilation system, consult with an HVAC professional to install an ERV. This will ensure that fresh, dry air is being introduced while stale, moist air is exhausted. 3. **Assess occupant-generated moisture:** Reduce internal sources of moisture by running exhaust fans during showers and while cooking. Ensure these fans vent directly to the outside, not into an attic or crawlspace. 4. **Install a whole-house dehumidifier:** If ventilation is insufficient, add a whole-house dehumidifier. These units can be ducted into your existing HVAC system to distribute dry air evenly throughout the home. 5. **Monitor and calibrate:** Once the system is installed, monitor your humidity levels for two weeks. Adjust the dehumidifier setpoint until you reach a consistent level between 40% and 50%.

How do I manage winter dryness? Ironically, while summer brings high humidity, the same airtight construction can lead to excessive dryness in the winter. This occurs because the air exchange rate is low and the heating system further reduces the relative humidity of the indoor air. To combat this, ensure your ventilation system has a control that allows you to reduce the air exchange rate when outdoor air is extremely cold and dry, or consider a whole-house humidifier if levels consistently drop below 30%.
