Why colorectal cancer is rising in adults under 50

By Dr. Sarah Jenkins • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Colorectal cancer is rising in adults under 50. Learn why you should not ignore symptoms like rectal bleeding or bowel changes, regardless of your age.

Outline

  • The reality of early-onset colorectal cancer
  • Shifting demographics and risk factors
  • Understanding the screening guidelines
  • Symptoms that require immediate attention
  • How to advocate for your health

Colorectal cancer is no longer an older adult’s disease. While the standard age for routine screening in the United States is 45, the incidence of colorectal cancer is rising rapidly among adults in their 20s, 30s, and early 40s. If you experience persistent gastrointestinal symptoms, you should seek a diagnostic evaluation immediately, regardless of your age or perceived risk profile.

According to data from the American Cancer Society, the rate of new colorectal cancer cases among adults younger than 50 has increased by about 1% to 2% annually since the mid-1990s. This shift has prompted a critical re-evaluation of how doctors diagnose patients who fall outside the traditional age demographic for this malignancy.

The reality of early-onset colorectal cancer

For decades, colorectal cancer was viewed as a condition that primarily affected individuals over the age of 60. This clinical bias persists in some medical settings, often leading to delayed diagnoses for younger patients. Cases like that of Azucena Reyes, who was told at age 32 that she was "too young" for the disease, illustrate the danger of relying solely on age-based risk assessment.

When symptoms occur in younger adults, they are frequently misattributed to more common, benign conditions such as hemorrhoids, irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), or dietary sensitivities. This diagnostic delay is particularly concerning because early-onset colorectal cancer is often more aggressive and frequently diagnosed at a later stage, such as stage 3 or 4, compared to cancers detected through routine screening in older adults.

Shifting demographics and risk factors

Recent epidemiological research indicates that while colorectal cancer rates are increasing across all racial and ethnic groups under age 50, the surge is particularly pronounced among Hispanic and Latino populations in the United States. Researchers are currently investigating the environmental, dietary, and genetic drivers of this trend.

Beyond ethnicity, lifestyle factors that have become more common in younger generations—such as sedentary behavior, high intake of processed foods, and rising rates of obesity—are being studied as potential contributors. However, it is essential to note that many young patients diagnosed with the disease have no conventional risk factors. This underscores the importance of listening to your body when something feels "off," rather than assuming you are protected by a healthy lifestyle or a lack of family history.

Understanding the screening guidelines

In 2021, the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) officially lowered the recommended starting age for colorectal cancer screening to 45. This decision was a direct response to the documented increase in early-onset cases. However, this screening protocol is designed for average-risk individuals who are asymptomatic.

If you have a personal or family history of colorectal polyps or cancer, or if you carry specific genetic mutations such as Lynch syndrome, your screening timeline will be much more aggressive. In these cases, doctors often recommend starting screenings a decade before the age at which your youngest relative was diagnosed. If you have a family history, consult with a gastroenterologist to establish a surveillance plan that starts well before age 45.

Symptoms that require immediate attention

Because routine screening is not standard for most people under 45, symptom awareness is your primary line of defense. You should schedule an appointment with a primary care physician or a gastroenterologist if you experience any of the following "red flag" symptoms for more than a few weeks:

* **Rectal bleeding:** This can manifest as bright red blood in the stool or on toilet paper. Never assume this is solely due to hemorrhoids. * **Persistent changes in bowel habits:** This includes new-onset constipation, diarrhea, or a feeling that your bowel is not emptying completely. * **Stool changes:** Stools that are consistently narrower than usual (often called "pencil-thin stools"). * **Abdominal discomfort:** Ongoing cramping, gas, or pain that does not resolve with dietary changes or over-the-counter medication. * **Unexplained systemic issues:** Unintentional weight loss, persistent fatigue, or symptoms of anemia (such as dizziness or shortness of breath) that occur without a clear cause.

How to advocate for your health

If you are under 45 and present with these symptoms, you may encounter resistance from healthcare providers who default to the "you are too young" narrative. To advocate for yourself, be precise and persistent. Document your symptoms in a journal, noting the frequency, duration, and specific nature of your discomfort.

When speaking with a physician, use clear language: "I am experiencing [symptom] and I am concerned about the possibility of colorectal cancer. I would like to discuss diagnostic options, such as a colonoscopy or a fecal immunochemical test (FIT)." If a doctor dismisses your concerns without a thorough investigation or a referral to a specialist, seek a second opinion. When it comes to your health, your intuition combined with persistent data collection is your most effective tool for securing a timely diagnosis.
