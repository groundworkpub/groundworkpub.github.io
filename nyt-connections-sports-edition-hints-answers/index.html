How to solve the NYT connections sports edition puzzle

By Groundwork Editorial • 3 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Learn the strategy for solving NYT Connections: Sports Edition. Master categorization, spot linguistic traps, and improve your daily puzzle performance.

In This Guide

  • How to approach the grid
  • Decoding thematic categories
  • Dealing with linguistic patterns
  • Best practices for long-term improvement

NYT Connections is a daily word-categorization game where players must organize 16 words into four groups of four based on shared, often subtle, thematic links. The Sports Edition variant applies this logic specifically to athletic terminology, team histories, and industry-related vocabulary. Mastering these puzzles requires a combination of lateral thinking, sports trivia knowledge, and the ability to spot linguistic patterns like anagrams.

How to approach the grid

To solve a Connections puzzle effectively, you must first scan the 16 words for obvious overlaps. Often, the game includes "red herrings"—words that seem to fit into two different categories to intentionally mislead you. According to the New York Times game design documentation, each word has exactly one correct placement within the four designated groups.

1. Analyze the board for obvious associations: Look for proper nouns, verb forms, or specific sports terminology that stand out. 2. Identify the categories by theme: Groupings can be based on factual data (like team names) or linguistic properties (like anagrams). 3. Test your connections: If you choose four words and receive an "incorrect" notification, analyze why those words might have been grouped together incorrectly. This helps you identify the "trap" words. 4. Save the most difficult category for last: The game ranks categories by difficulty, with yellow being the easiest and purple being the most challenging. If you are stuck, solving the easier categories first will narrow your remaining options.

Decoding thematic categories

Categories in the Sports Edition often rely on specific domain knowledge. For example, a puzzle might focus on "Teams that have never won a World Series," which requires historical baseball knowledge. Other common category types include:

* **Industry roles:** Occupations associated with a specific sport, such as "caddie" or "greenskeeper" in golf. These require an understanding of how a sports facility operates behind the scenes. * **Regional clusters:** Groups of teams that represent the same city or geographic area, such as the Kansas City Chiefs, Royals, Current, and Sporting KC. * **Linguistic puzzles:** Categories like anagrams (rearranging letters to form a new word) or homophones. These categories test your ability to look at words as shapes and letters rather than just meanings.

Dealing with linguistic patterns

When a category feels impossible, it is often because the answer is hidden in the structure of the words themselves rather than their definition. Anagrams are a staple of the purple (hardest) category in Connections. For instance, if you see words like "case" and "madre," look for hidden WNBA team names like "Aces" or "Dream."

Research from cognitive psychologists suggests that "functional fixedness" is the biggest barrier in these games—the tendency to only see a word by its primary definition. To break this, try reading the words aloud to listen for hidden rhymes or phonetic similarities. If you are struggling, step away from the screen for ten minutes. Cognitive rest often allows the brain to reset its pattern-matching pathways, making the connection suddenly obvious upon your return.

Best practices for long-term improvement

Developing a "Connections strategy" involves more than just knowing sports trivia. It requires methodical elimination.

* **Track your mistakes:** If you lose a "life" by guessing incorrectly, take a screenshot of the grid before you proceed. This allows you to review the board layout later to see which words you miscategorized. * **Use the shuffle button:** The default layout is designed to group related words together visually to trick your brain. Using the shuffle button regularly forces your eyes to see new combinations. * **Build a knowledge base:** Regularly reading sports history or trivia can reduce the cognitive load required to identify team-based categories, leaving you more mental energy to solve the complex linguistic categories.
