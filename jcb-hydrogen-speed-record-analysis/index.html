How hydrogen combustion engines reached record-breaking speeds

By Alex Rivera • 3 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

JCB's recent 406 mph record highlights how hydrogen combustion engines are evolving. Learn how this tech works and its role in decarbonizing heavy industry.

Outline

  • The shift toward hydrogen combustion
  • Challenges of hydrogen as a fuel
  • Engineering the record-breaking Hydromax
  • Practical steps for hydrogen adoption

A hydrogen combustion engine is an internal combustion engine modified to burn hydrogen fuel instead of gasoline or diesel. The JCB Hydromax recently demonstrated the potential of this technology by setting a land speed record of 406 mph on the Bonneville Salt Flats, proving that hydrogen can serve as a high-performance alternative to traditional fossil fuels in heavy-duty applications.

The shift toward hydrogen combustion

JCB, a manufacturer primarily known for construction and agricultural machinery, faced increasing pressure to meet UK carbon emission targets. While battery-electric power is a viable solution for some light-duty equipment, the company found that hydrogen fuel cells were often too fragile and costly for the harsh environments of a construction site (Ars Technica, 2026). Consequently, JCB pivoted to hydrogen combustion. Unlike fuel cells, which convert hydrogen into electricity to power an electric motor, hydrogen combustion engines burn the gas directly, similar to how a standard engine burns gasoline. This approach retains the mechanical simplicity and durability of traditional internal combustion engines while significantly lowering carbon output.

Challenges of hydrogen as a fuel

Historically, burning hydrogen in an engine has been difficult due to the fuel's unique physical properties. Hydrogen has a lower energy density by volume than gasoline, meaning it requires more space to store and delivers less power per combustion cycle if the engine is not specifically engineered to handle it. In 2006, BMW experimented with a hydrogen-powered V12 engine in a 7 Series, but the results were suboptimal; the vehicle suffered from a 60 percent reduction in power and significantly higher fuel consumption compared to its gasoline counterpart (Ars Technica, 2026). To overcome these limitations, modern developers like Toyota and JCB have utilized forced induction, such as turbochargers, to increase the amount of air and fuel forced into the cylinders, effectively compensating for the fuel's lower energy density.

Engineering the record-breaking Hydromax

The JCB Hydromax achieved its 406 mph record by utilizing two hydrogen-burning engines working in tandem. By focusing on internal combustion, JCB avoided the complex cooling and sensitive membranes required by fuel cell stacks, which are prone to failure under the vibration and dust typical of heavy industrial work. The success of this record-breaking run indicates that hydrogen combustion is not merely a theoretical curiosity but a practical path for decarbonizing heavy machinery. While current land speed records like the 763.6 mph set by Thrust SSC in 1997 remain far out of reach for hydrogen vehicles, the Hydromax establishes a new benchmark for clean-burning speed (Ars Technica, 2026).

Practical steps for hydrogen adoption

If you are evaluating hydrogen technology for industrial or commercial use, consider the following steps:

1. Assess your duty cycle: Determine if your equipment operates in environments where battery-electric charging infrastructure is impractical or where equipment durability is the primary concern. 2. Evaluate engine longevity: Compare the maintenance requirements of fuel cell stacks versus hydrogen combustion engines. Combustion engines are generally more familiar to existing maintenance crews. 3. Analyze fuel availability: Ensure that your regional supply chain can support hydrogen fuel delivery, as storage and transport remain significant logistical hurdles for hydrogen adoption. 4. Review emission requirements: Consult with local environmental agencies to determine if hydrogen combustion meets the specific "net-zero" or "low-carbon" standards applicable to your industry sector.

By leveraging existing mechanical expertise and focusing on robust combustion technology, manufacturers are finding that hydrogen can bridge the gap between legacy diesel power and a carbon-neutral future.
