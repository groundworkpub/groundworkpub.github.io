How socioeconomic status influences multimorbidity and healthcare costs in older adults

Multimorbidity is the co-occurrence of two or more chronic health conditions in a single individual. Among adults aged 65 and older, this condition is not only a significant health challenge but also a primary driver of rising public health and social care expenditures. Recent data from a large-scale population study in Stockholm, Sweden, indicates that multimorbidity affects 62.6% of the elderly population, with lower-income groups experiencing the onset of these conditions at younger ages than their higher-income counterparts.

Why does socioeconomic status matter for chronic health? Socioeconomic status serves as a proxy for the cumulative exposure to environmental, behavioral, and occupational risks throughout a person's life. Individuals in lower income brackets are statistically more likely to face barriers to preventative care, higher levels of chronic stress, and limited access to healthy living environments. According to research published in BMJ Open (2024), these disparities lead to an earlier onset of chronic diseases, effectively shortening the period of healthy aging and increasing the duration of time spent living with multiple health conditions.

How does multimorbidity affect healthcare and social care spending? Multimorbidity is the most significant predictor of high health and social care costs in older populations. As the number of chronic conditions increases, the financial burden on the individual and the state rises exponentially. For example, individuals with no chronic conditions incur significantly lower costs compared to those managing four or more conditions. The data suggests that once a patient reaches a threshold of four or more chronic diseases, the annual expenditure can climb as high as 143,063 SEK (Swedish Kronor), compared to roughly 34,346 SEK for those with no chronic conditions.

What is the role of social care in the aging population? Social care—which includes assistance with daily living activities, home help, and long-term nursing support—becomes the dominant component of total expenditure as patients age. While primary and specialty healthcare costs are high during the initial diagnosis and management phases of chronic disease, social care costs begin to surpass medical expenditure after age 80. This shift highlights a critical gap in many healthcare systems: the transition from clinical treatment to long-term support. Policymakers must recognize that as life expectancy increases, the demand for non-medical social support services will likely outpace the demand for traditional hospital-based care.

How can you manage the financial risks of multimorbidity? Managing the financial impact of chronic conditions requires a proactive approach to health. While socioeconomic factors are often outside of an individual’s immediate control, evidence-based health strategies can help delay the onset or severity of secondary conditions.

1. Prioritize preventative screenings: Early detection of conditions like hypertension or diabetes allows for management before they escalate into complex multimorbidity. 2. Focus on functional mobility: Maintaining physical strength and balance is the most effective way to delay the need for intensive social care services. 3. Evaluate long-term care insurance: Because social care costs are a significant financial risk after age 80, investigate whether long-term care insurance or savings plans are necessary for your specific financial situation. 4. Optimize medication management: Polypharmacy—the use of multiple medications—is a common complication of multimorbidity. Work with your primary care provider to ensure your treatment plan is streamlined and that you are not taking redundant or unnecessary prescriptions.

What are the systemic challenges for future healthcare planning? Healthcare systems globally are struggling to adapt to an aging population, and the current evidence suggests that a reactive model of care is becoming unsustainable. The association between lower income and higher expenditure suggests that current systems are failing to provide adequate preventative support to the most vulnerable. To address this, healthcare organizations are shifting focus toward 'integrated care' models. These models aim to coordinate care across clinical, social, and psychological domains, ensuring that patients receive support before their health reaches a crisis point.

For the individual, this means advocating for your own care coordination. If you are managing multiple conditions, ensure that your primary physician and any specialists are communicating effectively. Fragmented care often leads to duplicate testing and conflicting treatment protocols, both of which increase costs and decrease the quality of your health outcomes. By taking a proactive, informed stance on your medical management, you can navigate the complexities of a system that is currently struggling to keep pace with the needs of an aging society.
