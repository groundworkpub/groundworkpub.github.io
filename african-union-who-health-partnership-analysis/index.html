How the renewed AU and WHO partnership impacts African health systems

By Groundwork Editorial • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

The AU and WHO have renewed a strategic partnership to shift African health systems from aid dependency toward sustainable, regional health sovereignty.

What is the AU-WHO strategic partnership?

The African Union (AU) and World Health Organization (WHO) strategic partnership is a formal framework for cooperation designed to integrate regional health policy with global technical expertise. The 2025 Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) serves as a roadmap for AU member states to transition from aid-dependent health models to sustainable, self-reliant health systems, specifically targeting health security, universal health coverage, and local pharmaceutical manufacturing.

According to the WHO, this agreement marks a shift in focus from short-term emergency response to long-term systemic resilience. By aligning the AU’s political mandate with the WHO’s normative standards, the partnership aims to stabilize health infrastructure during a period of declining bilateral aid, which the WHO reports has left millions vulnerable to service gaps.

Why is this partnership critical for African health sovereignty?

Health sovereignty is the ability of a nation or region to manage, fund, and maintain its own health systems without reliance on external aid. The renewed AU-WHO partnership prioritizes this transition because external health funding is increasingly volatile, with many donor countries scaling back commitments to redirect resources toward domestic priorities or other geopolitical concerns.

Evidence from the AU Commission suggests that "budgeting for survival"—relying on fluctuating international grants—prevents long-term investments in essential infrastructure. By consolidating the AU’s political leadership and the WHO’s technical guidance, the partnership aims to standardize regulatory frameworks across the continent. This harmonization is essential for creating a predictable environment for private sector investment and local pharmaceutical production, effectively reducing the continent’s reliance on imported medical supplies.

How does the agreement strengthen health systems?

Strengthening health systems under the new MoU involves a multi-pronged approach focused on institutional capacity and resource mobilization. The partnership identifies five primary pillars for intervention, each designed to address specific systemic weaknesses that have historically hindered public health outcomes in the region.

1. **Regulatory harmonization:** Establishing unified standards for medicines and medical devices across African borders to streamline supply chains. 2. **Local pharmaceutical manufacturing:** Providing technical support to scale up domestic production, reducing the cost and lead time for essential medications. 3. **Traditional medicine integration:** Formally incorporating evidence-based traditional medicine into the primary healthcare sector to increase accessibility. 4. **Domestic health financing:** Developing sustainable revenue streams through national health insurance schemes and increased government health allocations. 5. **Workforce development:** Scaling training programs for healthcare workers, including community health professionals, to bridge the gap in service delivery.

These steps are designed to be implemented by the AU’s Department of Health, Humanitarian Affairs, and Social Development, ensuring that health policy is not siloed but integrated into broader humanitarian and economic development strategies.

What are the practical implications for health policy implementation?

For policymakers and stakeholders, this partnership shifts the implementation focus from the WHO acting as a primary service provider to acting as a technical advisor to AU-led initiatives. This change is intended to make health programs more culturally relevant and politically sustainable within the African context.

Data from the World Bank indicates that countries with robust regional regulatory frameworks see a faster uptake of medical innovations and improved response times to health emergencies. By leveraging the AU’s political leverage, the partnership expects to accelerate the adoption of these frameworks. The focus is on moving away from fragmented, donor-driven projects toward integrated national health plans that are owned and managed by the countries themselves.

How should stakeholders measure progress?

Measuring the success of this partnership requires looking beyond initial funding commitments. Instead, progress should be evaluated based on the transition from aid-based funding to domestic health financing. Key performance indicators for this initiative include:

* **Domestic health expenditure ratios:** The percentage of national budgets allocated to health, as tracked by the AU. * **Regulatory approval times:** The speed at which new, essential medicines receive approval through harmonized regional systems. * **Manufacturing capacity:** The number of AU-certified local pharmaceutical facilities producing essential medicines, such as vaccines and antibiotics. * **Universal Health Coverage (UHC) index:** The proportion of the population with access to essential services without financial hardship.

These metrics provide a clear benchmark for whether the partnership is achieving its stated goal of "health sovereignty." If the AU and WHO succeed in these areas, the region will be better equipped to handle future pandemics and chronic health burdens without the volatility associated with international aid cycles.

Next steps for regional health stakeholders

If you are involved in public health, policy, or humanitarian sectors, the primary takeaway is that the landscape is shifting toward regional self-reliance. You should monitor the specific regulatory changes emerging from the AU’s Department of Health, as these will likely dictate the standards for medical procurement and service delivery in the coming decade. Prioritize alignment with regional standards over individual donor-led models to ensure long-term project viability.
