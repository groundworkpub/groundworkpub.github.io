Understanding the long-term impact of moral injury on healthcare workers

By Groundwork Editorial • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Moral injury in healthcare is a long-term psychological burden caused by systemic constraints. Learn how institutional betrayal compounds this trauma.

In This Guide

  • What is moral injury in a clinical setting?
  • How do systemic factors contribute to moral injury?
  • What is the 'secondary betrayal' experienced by healthcare workers?
  • How does moral injury manifest in professional responses?
  • What can be done to mitigate the impact of moral injury?

Moral injury is the psychological distress resulting from actions, or the lack of them, that violate one’s own moral or ethical code. In the context of the National Health Service (NHS), healthcare workers (HCWs) frequently encounter potentially morally injurious events (PMIEs)—situations where they feel unable to provide the standard of care they believe is necessary due to systemic constraints. At Groundwork, our analysis of longitudinal research confirms that these experiences are not fleeting; they evolve over time, often compounded by a sense of 'secondary betrayal' from the institutions and systems that workers serve.

What is moral injury in a clinical setting?

Moral injury is a profound psychological response to witnessing or participating in events that transgress deeply held moral beliefs. Unlike burnout, which is characterized by exhaustion and emotional depletion due to chronic workplace stress, moral injury is rooted in the erosion of a professional’s integrity. When a clinician is forced to prioritize administrative efficiency over patient safety, or when resource scarcity prevents the delivery of equitable care, the resulting dissonance creates a lasting cognitive and emotional burden. Research indicates that this is not merely a personal failing but a systemic outcome of rigid organizational structures that limit individual agency.

How do systemic factors contribute to moral injury?

Systemic constraints are the primary drivers of moral injury within healthcare environments. When healthcare workers operate within a rigid system, they often experience a loss of control over their clinical decision-making. This lack of autonomy is a critical factor in the development of moral injury. Our synthesis of the evidence shows that when clinicians perceive that their work environment does not value their professional judgment or patient outcomes, the result is a breakdown in psychological safety. This environment fosters a culture where staff feel forced to choose between the 'right' thing to do and the 'possible' thing to do, leading to sustained psychological distress.

What is the 'secondary betrayal' experienced by healthcare workers?

Secondary betrayal occurs when the individuals or organizations that a worker trusts to support them fail to acknowledge their sacrifices or, worse, minimize the systemic failures that led to their distress. A recent three-year follow-up study of NHS staff highlights that moral injury is not static. While the initial trauma may stem from acute clinical events—such as those experienced during the COVID-19 pandemic—the long-term damage is frequently exacerbated by a perceived lack of governmental and organizational recognition. This feeling of betrayal stems from the disconnect between the public rhetoric of 'heroism' and the reality of ongoing neglect, lack of resource support, and the refusal of leadership to acknowledge systemic mistakes.

How does moral injury manifest in professional responses?

Healthcare workers respond to PMIEs through a spectrum of adaptive and maladaptive behaviors. Adaptive responses may include seeking peer support, engaging in reflective practice, or advocating for systemic change. Conversely, maladaptive responses often manifest as moral disengagement, emotional numbing, or an exit from the profession entirely. At Groundwork, we identify that the transition between these states is heavily influenced by the organizational culture. If an institution fosters transparency and psychological safety, workers are more likely to process these events adaptively. Without such support, the cumulative effect of unresolved moral injury can lead to long-term mental health challenges, including depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress symptoms.

What can be done to mitigate the impact of moral injury?

Addressing moral injury requires a shift from individual-focused resilience training to structural, organizational reform. Interventions must move beyond 'wellness apps' or mindfulness sessions, which often place the burden of recovery on the individual. Instead, evidence-based mitigation strategies should focus on the following:

1. Institutional accountability: Organizations must acknowledge specific systemic failures and create clear, non-punitive channels for reporting ethical concerns. 2. Restoring professional autonomy: Returning decision-making power to frontline clinicians regarding patient care standards reduces the dissonance that leads to moral injury. 3. Transparent communication: Leaders must engage in honest dialogue about the limitations of the system, replacing performative gratitude with tangible investments in staff well-being. 4. Peer-support networks: Establishing protected time for clinical debriefing allows workers to process moral dilemmas collectively, reducing the isolation that often deepens the experience of betrayal.

By focusing on these structural pillars, healthcare organizations can begin to restore the trust necessary to support their workforce. At Groundwork, our research framework emphasizes that until the gap between the moral demands of the clinical role and the reality of the organizational environment is bridged, the risk of moral injury will remain a pervasive challenge for healthcare professionals.
