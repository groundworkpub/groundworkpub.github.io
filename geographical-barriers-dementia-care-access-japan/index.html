Geographical barriers to dementia care access in Japan

By Groundwork Editorial • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Geographical barriers like heavy snowfall and mountainous terrain limit dementia care access in Japan. Learn how to navigate these gaps and find reliable care.

In This Guide

  • What is a medical centre for dementia? A medical centre for dementia (MCD) is a specialized facility designated by the Japanese government to provide expert diagnosis, treatment, and support for dementia patients. Japan’s national policy aims to establish at least one MCD in every secondary healthcare area (SHA) to ensure that residents, regardless of their location, have equitable access to specialized neurological care and support services.
  • How does geography impact access to dementia care? Geography is a primary determinant of healthcare access in Japan, where mountainous terrain, heavy snowfall, and remote island locations create significant barriers to reaching specialized dementia services. Research indicates that even when policy mandates one center per secondary healthcare area, physical distance and environmental conditions often prevent patients in rural or disadvantaged areas from utilizing these facilities (BMJ Open, 2024).
  • The impact of heavy snowfall and mountainous terrain Heavy snowfall and mountainous geography are among the most significant physical obstacles to healthcare access in Japan. During winter months, severe weather conditions can effectively isolate municipalities, making travel to an MCD dangerous or impossible. A recent geocomputational analysis found that 109 out of 509 identified MCDs (roughly 20%) are located in regions characterized by heavy or extremely heavy snowfall. While these centers exist, the driving time to reach them often exceeds reasonable limits when road conditions are compromised by snow or complex, winding mountain topography.
  • Challenges in remote islands and peninsulas Remote islands and peninsulas present unique logistical challenges that go beyond simple road distance. These areas often lack the infrastructure to support specialized medical facilities, leading to a reliance on "affiliated" centers that may not offer the same level of diagnostic sophistication as "core" or "regional" centers. Data shows that only 2.2% of all MCDs are located on remote islands, forcing residents to travel by ferry or other restricted transport methods to reach mainland facilities. This physical distance often delays early diagnosis, which is critical for managing dementia progression.
  • Are there gaps in the current dementia care network? Despite a national mandate for universal coverage, approximately 5.2% of secondary healthcare areas in Japan currently lack a dedicated medical centre for dementia. This gap affects 105 municipalities, primarily those in geographically isolated or climatically harsh environments. When an SHA lacks an MCD, the local dementia care pathway often becomes fragmented, with municipalities either designating centers in distant adjacent areas or failing to provide clear guidance to residents at all.
  • Analyzing the 30-minute catchment area To understand real-world accessibility, researchers use "isochrone analysis," which maps the area reachable within a specific time, typically 30 minutes of driving. In many municipalities lacking a local MCD, the 30-minute catchment window is severely restricted by geographical barriers. A large lake, a mountain range, or a peninsula can turn a 15-kilometer straight-line distance into a two-hour drive. This reality means that administrative coverage—having a center "in the prefecture"—does not equate to clinical accessibility for the patient.

If you or a loved one live in an area with limited access to a specialized medical centre for dementia, proactive planning is essential to ensure consistent care. First, verify your local dementia care pathway by contacting your municipal health office and asking specifically about the nearest designated facility. Do not assume the closest facility is the one listed in your general health documents. Second, inquire about telehealth options; many regional centers are now expanding remote consultation services for patients who struggle with transportation. Finally, seek out local dementia support groups or community-based welfare centers, as these organizations often maintain updated, practical knowledge about the most reliable transport routes and alternative care options in your specific region. Prioritizing early, consistent communication with your municipal health department is the most effective way to navigate these geographical limitations.

How can regional dementia care planning be improved? To achieve truly equitable access, future healthcare planning must shift from simple administrative "per-area" quotas to a more nuanced model that accounts for topographical and climatic constraints. Policymakers should prioritize the following steps to bridge the gap in underserved regions:

1. Conduct geocomputational audits: Local governments should map their dementia care pathways against actual travel times rather than political boundaries. 2. Implement mobile diagnostic units: In areas where building a permanent MCD is not feasible due to population density or terrain, mobile assessment teams can provide initial screenings. 3. Utilize telemedicine for remote follow-ups: For patients who have already received an initial diagnosis, integrating telehealth can reduce the burden of travel for routine check-ups and medication management. 4. Strengthen local primary care partnerships: Since MCDs are distant, primary care physicians in isolated areas must be better equipped to provide interim dementia support, acting as a bridge between the patient and the regional center.

If you or a loved one live in an area with limited access to a specialized medical centre for dementia, proactive planning is essential to ensure consistent care. First, verify your local dementia care pathway by contacting your municipal health office and asking specifically about the nearest designated facility. Do not assume the closest facility is the one listed in your general health documents.
