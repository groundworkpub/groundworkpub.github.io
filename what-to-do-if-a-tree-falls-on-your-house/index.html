What to do if a tree falls on your house

By David Sterling • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

A tree falling on your house is a structural emergency. Follow this guide to secure your family, mitigate further damage, and navigate your insurance claim.

Outline

  • How do you ensure immediate safety after a tree falls?
  • Who should you contact first after a tree falls?
  • How can you prevent further damage to your home?
  • How does the insurance claims process work for tree damage?
  • When is it safe to return to your home?

A tree falling on your home is a major structural emergency that requires immediate safety precautions and a systematic approach to restoration. The process begins with ensuring occupant safety, followed by utility assessment, and concludes with professional damage mitigation and insurance documentation. According to industry data, tree-related claims account for approximately 8 percent of property insurance filings, highlighting that while such events are unexpected, they are a frequent occurrence for homeowners (Source: Bob Vila).

How do you ensure immediate safety after a tree falls?

The immediate priority is to account for all household members and pets, moving them to a secure location away from the structure. Once safety is confirmed, you must assess for secondary hazards such as downed power lines or gas leaks, which frequently accompany structural impact from falling trees.

1. Evacuate all occupants and pets to a safe distance, at least 350 feet away, if you suspect a gas leak or if the home is visibly unstable. 2. Do not re-enter the home until you have verified there are no electrical or gas hazards. 3. If you detect a gas odor or hear hissing, leave immediately, do not touch light switches, and call 911 from a distance. 4. Check for downed power lines that may be tangled in the tree or touching the structure; treat all downed lines as live and lethal. 5. If it is safe to do so, shut off the main electrical breaker to prevent fire hazards caused by damaged internal wiring.

Who should you contact first after a tree falls?

After ensuring human safety, contact your utility providers and emergency services to secure the site. Professional intervention is required to manage live wires and structural instability, and you should not attempt to remove the tree yourself, as this can lead to further collapse or injury.

Notify your local electrical utility company immediately if you observe downed lines. If you suspect a gas leak, contact your gas provider or local fire department. Once the immediate scene is secured, your next call should be to your homeowners insurance agent to open a claim. Documenting the scene before any cleanup begins is essential for your claim, so capture high-resolution photographs and videos of the damage from multiple angles. Do not sign contracts with "storm chasers" or door-to-door repair services; rely on licensed, insured professionals recommended by your insurance carrier or vetted local contractors (Source: Insurance Information Institute).

How can you prevent further damage to your home?

Once the area is declared safe by professionals, your goal is to mitigate "secondary damage" such as water intrusion, which can lead to mold and rot. Most insurance policies require you to take reasonable steps to protect the property from further loss after a covered event.

If the roof or walls are compromised, cover the affected areas with heavy-duty tarps. If you cannot reach the roof safely, hire a professional roofing contractor to install emergency weatherproofing. Remove standing water if possible, and use dehumidifiers or industrial fans to dry out interior spaces to prevent mold growth. Keep all receipts for emergency repairs, materials, and temporary housing, as these are often reimbursable under the "loss of use" or "dwelling protection" provisions of your homeowners insurance policy.

How does the insurance claims process work for tree damage?

Your homeowners insurance typically covers the cost of tree removal and structural repairs if the tree fell due to a covered peril, such as wind, lightning, or hail. However, coverage specifics depend on whether the tree was on your property or your neighbor's property, and whether the tree was healthy or neglected.

If a tree falls on your house, your policy generally covers the damage to the structure and the cost of removing the tree from the home. If the tree fell from a neighbor's yard, your insurance company will often handle the claim and may subrogate—or seek reimbursement—from the neighbor’s insurance if negligence can be proven, such as if the tree was dead and the neighbor failed to remove it after being warned. If the tree fell on your property but did not hit the house, coverage for removal is often limited or excluded unless it blocks a driveway or a handicap ramp. Review your declarations page to understand your specific deductible and coverage limits for debris removal.

When is it safe to return to your home?

Do not attempt to inhabit or occupy the home until a licensed structural engineer or a qualified building inspector has verified that the frame and foundation remain sound. A tree may exert significant weight on structural rafters or load-bearing walls that are not immediately visible from the ground.

Structural damage can be deceptive. Even if the home appears intact, the impact may have shifted the foundation or compromised the integrity of the roof trusses. Wait for written confirmation from a professional before resuming normal activities inside the home. If your insurance adjuster has not yet visited, coordinate with them to ensure that any necessary demolition or cleanup does not interfere with their assessment of the damage. Always prioritize professional evaluations over the desire to return to your daily routine, as undetected structural failures can lead to catastrophic collapse later.
