Why cheap printers are a bad investment

A cheap printer is a low-cost hardware device often sold at or below the cost of production, designed to create long-term revenue through high-priced replacement ink cartridges. While these printers may seem like an economical choice for occasional home use, they frequently result in a higher cost-per-page and increased electronic waste compared to mid-range or business-class models.

According to data from industry analysts, the 'razor-and-blades' business model is the primary driver for printer pricing. Manufacturers sell the hardware at a loss or a very thin margin, banking on the fact that consumers will be locked into purchasing proprietary ink cartridges for the life of the machine. This strategy often makes a single set of replacement ink nearly as expensive as the printer itself.

Why does a cheap printer cost more in the long run?

A cheap printer costs more over time because the manufacturer subsidizes the hardware price by inflating the cost of ink cartridges. While a entry-level printer might retail for $40, the ink required to keep it functional over two years can easily exceed $200, depending on your printing volume.

Most budget printers use 'tri-color' cartridges, which combine cyan, magenta, and yellow into a single unit. If you run out of one color, you are forced to discard the entire cartridge, even if the other colors are still half-full. This design creates significant waste and forces you to replace the ink more frequently than printers that utilize individual ink tanks for each color. Furthermore, budget printers often lack efficiency features like automatic duplexing, which helps save on paper costs.

How can you calculate the true cost of a printer?

You can calculate the true cost of a printer by determining its cost-per-page (CPP). To find this, divide the price of a replacement ink cartridge by the manufacturer's stated page yield. Many budget printers have a high CPP, often exceeding 15 to 20 cents per page for monochrome documents, whereas laser printers or high-yield ink tank printers often bring this figure down to under 5 cents.

Follow these steps to evaluate your potential purchase: 1. Find the cost of a full set of replacement ink cartridges for the model. 2. Look up the official 'page yield' rating on the manufacturer's website. 3. Divide the total ink cost by the page yield to get the cost-per-page. 4. Compare this figure against the cost-per-page of a laser printer or an ink tank model.

Are ink tank printers a better alternative?

An ink tank printer is a more cost-effective alternative for most households because it replaces traditional cartridges with high-capacity reservoirs that you refill using bottles of ink. While these printers have a higher upfront cost, often starting around $200, the cost of ink refills is a fraction of what you would pay for cartridges.

Research indicates that ink tank printers can reduce your printing costs by up to 90% over the lifespan of the machine. Because you are buying ink in bulk rather than paying for the manufacturing and packaging of plastic cartridges, the environmental impact is also significantly reduced. For anyone who prints more than 50 pages per month, an ink tank printer will typically pay for itself within the first year of ownership.

Should you consider a laser printer instead?

If you primarily print monochrome documents, a laser printer is almost always the superior choice over an inkjet. Laser printers use toner, a dry powder that does not dry out like liquid ink, making them ideal for users who may go weeks or months without printing. While the initial purchase price is higher, the reliability and low cost-per-page make them a more sound financial decision for home offices.

When buying a printer, prioritize long-term operating costs over the initial sticker price. Check the cost of replacement toner or ink before you commit to a brand. If you find yourself replacing your printer every year because the ink is too expensive, you are effectively paying a premium for a cycle of waste.
