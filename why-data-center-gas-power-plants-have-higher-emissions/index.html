Why data center gas power plants have higher emissions

👉 Read the Full Study on Groundwork: Why data center gas power plants have higher emissions

A data center power plant is an on-site energy generation facility designed to provide consistent, high-capacity electricity to dedicated computing infrastructure. These facilities increasingly rely on simple-cycle gas turbines, which are less thermally efficient than the combined-cycle units typically found in the public electrical grid.

Recent data indicates that the environmental impact of these facilities is substantial. For example, a single Amazon-acquired project in Texas is permitted to emit up to 33 million tons of greenhouse gases annually, a scale that exceeds the emissions of dozens of average-sized natural gas power plants combined, according to U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) data cited by Wired.

Why do data centers use less efficient turbines?

Data centers prioritize rapid deployment and operational flexibility, leading developers to choose simple-cycle turbines over more efficient combined-cycle systems. Simple-cycle turbines function by mixing fuel and air in a combustion chamber to generate power, but they allow a significant amount of energy to escape as waste heat.

In contrast, combined-cycle units—the standard for utility-scale power plants—incorporate an additional steam turbine to capture that waste heat and convert it into supplemental electricity. By failing to recapture this heat, simple-cycle plants effectively burn more fuel to produce the same amount of power, resulting in a higher carbon footprint per megawatt generated. Companies often favor simple-cycle units because they are faster to install and can be ramped up or down quickly, providing the "instant-on" power required by high-demand AI workloads.

Which companies are deploying these systems?

Major technology firms are increasingly integrating on-site, gas-fired generation into their data center strategies to bypass grid constraints. This trend is not limited to a single firm; it is a systemic shift in how large-scale computing infrastructure is powered. Reports indicate that multiple major players are currently building or operating facilities dependent on simple-cycle technology:

1. Amazon: The company has acquired a large-scale project in Texas utilizing simple-cycle gas turbines. 2. Meta: The company is developing several power plants in Ohio through partnerships that rely on simple-cycle units. 3. Microsoft: Through a partnership with Crusoe, the company is building a facility in Abilene, Texas, powered by 39 simple-cycle turbines. 4. Google: Permit applications for the Goodnight data center in Texas indicate a reliance on 20 simple-cycle units. 5. xAI: The Colossus 1 and 2 data centers in Memphis utilize dozens of simple-cycle turbines for primary power.

What are the environmental implications?

The environmental cost of these facilities is measured by their permitted emission limits, which are significantly higher than traditional grid-connected plants. Even when a facility operates below its maximum permitted capacity, the sheer volume of fuel required for simple-cycle generation creates a massive aggregate increase in greenhouse gas output.

Industry analysts like Britt Burt of Industrial Info Resources note that the speed at which these power plants are being deployed has outpaced traditional grid infrastructure planning. While some companies argue that these on-site plants incorporate renewable elements like solar and battery storage, the primary load is still being carried by fossil fuel combustion. The lack of heat-recovery systems in these plants represents a regression in energy efficiency standards for large-scale industrial power generation.

How can data center energy impacts be mitigated?

To address the growing emissions profile of data centers, the industry must pivot toward more efficient power sourcing. If you are tracking the environmental impact of digital infrastructure, consider these steps:

1. Review local permit applications: Public databases provide data on the turbine types and emission limits for proposed industrial power projects in your region. 2. Evaluate PUE metrics: Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) is a standard metric, but it does not account for the source of the power. Demand transparency regarding the primary generation method. 3. Advocate for grid-scale efficiency: Supporting policies that mandate the use of combined-cycle or renewable-integrated systems can reduce the reliance on simple-cycle gas generation. 4. Monitor corporate sustainability reports: Compare stated "net-zero" goals against the actual energy generation methods disclosed in regulatory filings.
