How to protect your identity amid rising data breaches

A data breach is an unauthorized access event where sensitive, protected, or confidential data is copied, transmitted, viewed, or stolen by an individual unauthorized to do so. In the first half of 2026, the Identity Theft Resource Center (ITRC) recorded 1,803 data compromises, already exceeding the pace of previous years and signaling a systemic shift in how your personal information is targeted.

Recent data from the ITRC indicates that over 471 million victim notices were issued in the first half of 2026 alone, a figure already significantly higher than the 297.5 million notices reported throughout the entirety of 2025 (ITRC, 2026). This surge is driven by increasingly sophisticated attack vectors, including the integration of artificial intelligence by malicious actors.

How does artificial intelligence change the risk to your data? Artificial intelligence enables cybercriminals to exploit system vulnerabilities at a scale and speed that manual attacks cannot match. Research from IBM shows that between March 2025 and February 2026, one in four data breaches was AI-enabled, representing a 56% increase compared to the previous year (IBM, 2026). AI tools allow attackers to automate phishing attempts, create highly convincing deepfakes, and identify security gaps in software faster than human defenders can patch them.

Because these attacks are more efficient, companies are struggling to keep pace. While 93% of audit committees at public companies now rank cybersecurity as a top-three priority, the volume of incidents continues to grow, suggesting that internal corporate defenses are currently being outmaneuvered by automated, AI-driven threats (Deloitte/CAQ, 2025).

What are the signs that your data has been compromised? Identifying a breach early is the most effective way to prevent identity theft. You should monitor your financial accounts and credit reports for unauthorized activity, such as unexplained small transactions, new accounts you did not open, or notification emails from services you use stating that your login credentials have been changed.

Beyond financial accounts, be wary of "malicious insider" threats, which involve individuals within an organization intentionally misusing their access. The ITRC has noted a rise in these incidents, often linked to workforce instability or sophisticated job scams, which can bypass traditional cybersecurity perimeters entirely. If you receive a notification from a company regarding a data breach, treat it with urgency rather than dismissing it as spam.

How can you secure your personal information? Protecting your data requires a proactive, multi-layered approach to digital hygiene. Follow these steps to minimize your vulnerability:

1. Freeze your credit: Contact the three major credit bureaus—Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion—to place a security freeze on your credit files. This prevents unauthorized parties from opening new lines of credit in your name. 2. Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA): Use an authenticator app or a physical security key rather than SMS-based codes whenever possible. MFA adds a critical layer of friction that stops most automated credential-stuffing attacks. 3. Use a password manager: Generate unique, long, and complex passwords for every single account you own. Never reuse the same password across multiple platforms, as one breach will expose all your accounts simultaneously. 4. Monitor your accounts regularly: Set up real-time transaction alerts for all your bank accounts and credit cards so you are notified immediately of any suspicious activity. 5. Limit data sharing: Only provide the minimum amount of personal information required when signing up for services. Delete old accounts you no longer use to reduce your digital footprint.

What should you do if you are notified of a breach? If you receive a data breach notice, do not panic, but act immediately to contain the potential damage. First, change the password for the compromised account and any other accounts that share the same credentials. Second, check your credit report for unauthorized activity using official services like AnnualCreditReport.com. Finally, consider placing a fraud alert on your credit file, which requires businesses to verify your identity before extending new credit in your name. By treating every breach notification as a legitimate threat, you significantly reduce the window of opportunity for criminals to exploit your data.
