Understanding the currency premium puzzle and global interest rates

By Groundwork Editorial • 5 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

The currency premium puzzle highlights why standard economic models fail to predict interest rate differences between global currencies. Learn why this happens.

In This Guide

  • Why do standard economic models fail to explain currency premiums? Standard economic models fail to explain currency premiums because their internal mechanisms for balancing risk and return effectively immobilize interest rates, preventing them from diverging significantly across borders. These models are designed to solve the 'equity premium puzzle'—the observation that stocks historically outperform bonds—by balancing the mean and variance of the stochastic discount factor (the tool economists use to value future cash flows) in a way that keeps domestic interest rates stable (Hassan et al., 2024).

The stochastic discount factor (SDF) is the mathematical representation of how investors value future consumption versus current consumption, and it is the primary culprit in the currency premium puzzle. To achieve this balance, the model forces the mean of the SDF to offset its variance nearly one-for-one.

Many traditional asset pricing models assume 'complete markets,' where investors have access to every possible financial instrument to mitigate risk. In reality, global financial markets are incomplete; investors cannot easily trade away every type of idiosyncratic country-specific risk.

Recognize that exchange rates often fluctuate independently of interest rate changes, meaning your principal investment can lose value even if the interest rate is attractive. 3. Since economists have yet to fully explain the mechanics of currency premiums, it is safer to maintain a balanced portfolio rather than betting on specific interest rate spreads. 4. Focus on fundamental macroeconomic stability. When evaluating foreign assets, prioritize the underlying economic health of a country rather than relying solely on the yield offered by its currency.

A currency premium is the difference in expected returns between holding assets in different currencies, typically manifesting as higher interest rates in riskier currencies compared to safer ones. The currency premium puzzle refers to the persistent failure of standard economic models to explain why these interest rate differentials remain large and stable over time, even when accounting for investor risk preferences.

Recent research from the National Bureau of Economic Research highlights that traditional asset pricing models, which successfully explain stock market returns and domestic risk-free rates, break down when applied to international markets (Hassan, Mertens, & Wang, 2024). While these models accurately predict how investors demand compensation for risk within a single country, they struggle to map that same logic onto the global foreign exchange market.

Why do standard economic models fail to explain currency premiums? Standard economic models fail to explain currency premiums because their internal mechanisms for balancing risk and return effectively immobilize interest rates, preventing them from diverging significantly across borders. These models are designed to solve the 'equity premium puzzle'—the observation that stocks historically outperform bonds—by balancing the mean and variance of the stochastic discount factor (the tool economists use to value future cash flows) in a way that keeps domestic interest rates stable (Hassan et al., 2024).

When researchers apply this logic to an international context, the math forces a trade-off. To make the model work, it assumes that any difference in expected returns between currencies must be driven entirely by predictable movements in exchange rates. However, empirical data shows that exchange rates do not move in the predictable, systematic ways these models require. Instead, exchange rates are often volatile and disconnected from interest rate shifts, creating a fundamental gap between theoretical predictions and real-world market behavior.

What is the role of the stochastic discount factor in this puzzle? The stochastic discount factor (SDF) is the mathematical representation of how investors value future consumption versus current consumption, and it is the primary culprit in the currency premium puzzle. In a closed-economy model, the SDF is calibrated so that the average return on assets matches the observed risk premium. To achieve this balance, the model forces the mean of the SDF to offset its variance nearly one-for-one.

While this 'offset' is mathematically elegant for domestic assets, it creates a rigid environment for interest rates. Because the model is essentially locked into a specific relationship between risk and return, it cannot accommodate the large, persistent interest rate spreads observed between, for example, a stable currency like the Swiss Franc and a higher-yielding, riskier currency. Because the model cannot generate these spreads internally, it incorrectly forces the burden of adjustment onto exchange rate forecasts, which do not align with historical currency performance.

How does market incompleteness affect currency valuation? Market incompleteness refers to a scenario where investors cannot perfectly hedge all financial risks, and it is a critical factor in why currency premiums persist. Many traditional asset pricing models assume 'complete markets,' where investors have access to every possible financial instrument to mitigate risk. In reality, global financial markets are incomplete; investors cannot easily trade away every type of idiosyncratic country-specific risk.

Research suggests that even when you introduce market incompleteness and the 'exchange rate disconnect' (the observation that exchange rates often ignore macroeconomic fundamentals in the short term) into these models, the core tension remains (Hassan et al., 2024). The requirement that a country's risk-free asset must be priced by investors who demand compensation for risk creates a theoretical ceiling. Even with these modifications, the models still struggle to account for the magnitude of the interest rate differentials seen in global capital flows. This suggests that the current framework for understanding international finance may be missing a fundamental variable related to how global risk is priced and distributed.

Why does this matter for your financial decisions? Understanding the currency premium puzzle helps explain why simply chasing higher interest rates in foreign currencies—a strategy known as the 'carry trade'—is inherently risky and often disconnected from traditional economic theory. When you invest in a currency with a higher interest rate, you are effectively betting that the currency's value will not depreciate enough to wipe out the interest gains.

Because traditional models fail to explain why these premiums exist, they are also unreliable for predicting when these trades will fail. If the world's most sophisticated economic models cannot reconcile interest rate spreads with exchange rate movements, individual investors should be wary of relying on interest rate differentials as a primary indicator of currency strength or safety. The persistence of this puzzle indicates that currency markets are influenced by factors—such as liquidity constraints, geopolitical risk, and market segmentation—that remain poorly captured by standard quantitative models.

How should you interpret global interest rate data? 1. Acknowledge that interest rate differentials do not guarantee future returns. Higher interest rates are often a reflection of higher risk, not just a 'bonus' for holding that currency. 2. Monitor the 'exchange rate disconnect.' Recognize that exchange rates often fluctuate independently of interest rate changes, meaning your principal investment can lose value even if the interest rate is attractive. 3. Diversify your currency exposure. Since economists have yet to fully explain the mechanics of currency premiums, it is safer to maintain a balanced portfolio rather than betting on specific interest rate spreads. 4. Focus on fundamental macroeconomic stability. When evaluating foreign assets, prioritize the underlying economic health of a country rather than relying solely on the yield offered by its currency.
