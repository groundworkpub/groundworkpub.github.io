US courts will start reporting government spyware usage

A network investigative technique (NIT) is a form of government-authorized hacking used to intercept real-time communications from a target device. Starting in 2029, the U.S. judiciary will begin including specific data on the use of these tools in its annual Wiretap Report, providing the public with the first official count of how often federal authorities deploy spyware for surveillance.

Why are U.S. courts changing their reporting requirements? For decades, federal law enforcement has utilized hacking techniques and spyware to conduct investigations, but these actions have remained largely obscured from public view. The Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts (AO) manages annual Wiretap Reports, which detail surveillance activities authorized by judges, yet these reports have historically lacked a dedicated category for modern digital intrusion tools. Following pressure from lawmakers, including Senator Ron Wyden, the AO confirmed that it will update its reporting forms to capture "spyware/hacking" as a distinct surveillance method. This change is intended to improve oversight of invasive technologies that allow law enforcement to bypass traditional privacy protections by remotely accessing personal devices.

How does the current wiretap reporting process work? The U.S. judiciary operates under a structured system for tracking court-authorized surveillance. The Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts collects data from individual forms submitted by judges across the country throughout the calendar year. These reports break down surveillance by:

1. Jurisdiction: Whether the order was issued by federal or state-level judges. 2. Location: The specific states where the wiretaps were conducted. 3. Crime category: The nature of the investigation prompting the surveillance. 4. Communication type: Whether the tap targeted audio calls, oral conversations via microphones, or electronic messages like emails and texts.

By integrating spyware data into this existing workflow, the judiciary aims to standardize how "network investigative techniques" are counted alongside traditional wiretaps. The AO has noted that this transition requires updating procedural forms nationwide to ensure accuracy before the data appears in the 2028 report, scheduled for release in 2029.

What does this mean for your digital privacy? While this update marks a significant shift toward transparency, it is limited in scope. The new reporting requirement specifically tracks when spyware is used to intercept live communications, such as messages sent through encrypted services like Signal or WhatsApp. It does not necessarily account for other forms of remote phone hacking that may not involve real-time interception of communications. Wiretaps, by their nature, are highly invasive, often capturing large volumes of data from individuals who are not the targets of an investigation. For example, previous surveillance operations have resulted in the collection of millions of text messages over short periods. As the government increases its use of digital tools, this reporting mechanism will serve as a primary indicator of how frequently these powerful, intrusive technologies are deployed against the public.

How will this change the landscape of government surveillance? The addition of spyware statistics to the annual Wiretap Report will provide researchers, journalists, and the public with a baseline for understanding the scale of digital surveillance. Historically, the FBI has been using various hacking techniques since at least 1998, yet the lack of granular data has prevented a clear assessment of how often these tools are used. By formalizing this category, the judiciary is essentially acknowledging that NITs have become a standard, albeit invasive, tool in the modern law enforcement toolkit. This visibility is expected to facilitate more informed debates regarding the balance between national security interests and individual privacy rights, as the specific frequency of these deployments will no longer be a matter of speculation.
