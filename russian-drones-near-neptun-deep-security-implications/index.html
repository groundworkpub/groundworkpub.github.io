Security risks to European energy infrastructure: analyzing the Black Sea drone incidents

By Alex Rivera • 5 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

The discovery of Russian drones near Romania's Neptun Deep gas project highlights growing security risks to European energy infrastructure.

Outline

  • The strategic importance of the Neptun Deep project
  • Understanding the threat of hybrid warfare at sea
  • The vulnerability of subsea and surface energy assets
  • Strengthening maritime energy security

The discovery and subsequent destruction of Russian-made drones near Romania’s Neptun Deep offshore gas project represent a significant escalation in the vulnerability of European energy infrastructure. On August 11, Romanian military divers neutralized two Gerbera-type unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) found drifting in the Black Sea, marking a direct physical intersection between ongoing regional conflict and critical energy assets. As the European Union shifts its energy strategy to prioritize domestic production to offset historical reliance on Russian imports, the security of offshore platforms has moved from a routine operational concern to a matter of national and continental security.

According to data from the European Union Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators (ACER), the EU has invested over €50 billion in diversifying natural gas supply chains since 2022, making the protection of new sites like Neptun Deep essential for regional energy stability.

The strategic importance of the Neptun Deep project

Neptun Deep is positioned to be a cornerstone of European energy independence. Located in the Black Sea, this project is expected to transform Romania into the largest natural gas producer within the European Union. The site is designed to tap into vast reserves estimated at approximately 100 billion cubic meters of gas. For a continent that has spent the last three years aggressively decoupling from Russian pipeline gas, this project is not merely an economic venture; it is a strategic asset.

Energy infrastructure of this scale is inherently difficult to defend. Offshore platforms are static, exposed, and located in contested maritime zones. The presence of Russian-designed Gerbera drones—which are typically used for surveillance or as decoys—near this site suggests a reconnaissance mission aimed at mapping the vulnerabilities of the facility. When infrastructure is identified as a strategic chokepoint, it becomes a high-value target for hybrid warfare tactics, which seek to disrupt stability without triggering a full-scale military conflict.

Understanding the threat of hybrid warfare at sea

Hybrid warfare leverages non-traditional methods to exert pressure on an adversary. The use of drones in maritime environments is a quintessential example. Unlike traditional naval vessels, which are easily detected by long-range radar and satellite tracking, small-scale UAVs are difficult to identify and track. They can be deployed in large numbers to overwhelm defensive systems or, as seen in this incident, to conduct close-range inspection of critical maritime infrastructure.

Defense analysts note that the use of Gerbera-type drones, which are often produced inexpensively and in high volume, allows state actors to conduct persistent monitoring of sensitive sites. By drifting these devices near offshore platforms, actors can test the response times of coast guards, evaluate the effectiveness of acoustic and electronic detection systems, and signal intent to disrupt operations. For Romania, a NATO member, this incident forces a recalibration of how maritime security is managed. It is no longer sufficient to secure the platforms themselves; the entire surrounding maritime exclusion zone must be monitored with a level of vigilance previously reserved for active combat zones.

The vulnerability of subsea and surface energy assets

While the drones in this instance were found on the surface, the threat to offshore energy infrastructure extends beneath the waves. The 2022 sabotage of the Nord Stream pipelines demonstrated that energy infrastructure is highly susceptible to subsea interference. When you combine surface-level drone surveillance with the potential for underwater sabotage, the security profile of offshore rigs becomes significantly more complex.

Romania’s response—deploying army explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) divers—highlights the resource-intensive nature of neutralizing these threats. Every drone identified and destroyed requires a coordinated effort between the coast guard, military intelligence, and specialized dive teams. This operational burden acts as a form of 'gray zone' attrition, where the adversary incurs minimal costs by deploying cheap drones, while the defender must expend significant financial and human capital to maintain security. The confirmation from Ukrainian officials that the drones did not originate from their inventory reinforces the conclusion that these systems were deployed by Russian forces, signaling a deliberate intent to probe the security perimeters of a NATO-aligned state's energy infrastructure.

Strengthening maritime energy security

Moving forward, the protection of sites like Neptun Deep will likely require a multi-layered approach to defense. This includes:

* **Enhanced Electronic Warfare (EW) capabilities:** Deploying jamming technology to disrupt drone communication and GPS navigation within a specific radius of offshore assets. * **Integrated sensor networks:** Utilizing a combination of satellite imagery, acoustic sensors, and persistent aerial surveillance to detect small UAVs before they reach the facility’s immediate vicinity. * **Increased naval presence:** Regular, unpredictable patrols by coast guard and naval vessels to deter unauthorized approaches. * **International cooperation:** Sharing real-time intelligence with NATO partners to track the deployment and origin of unidentified maritime drones across the Black Sea.

For investors and stakeholders in the energy sector, this incident serves as a stark reminder that physical security is now a primary variable in the cost-benefit analysis of energy projects. You must account for the reality that critical energy infrastructure in Eastern Europe is operating within a contested security environment. As the EU continues to scale its domestic production to insulate itself from external shocks, the ability to protect these assets will determine the success or failure of the broader energy transition. The Romanian experience is a preview of the challenges that will likely characterize the next decade of European energy development.
