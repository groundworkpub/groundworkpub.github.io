How to buy land to build a house

👉 Read the Full Study on Groundwork: How to buy land to build a house

Buying land is the foundational step in building a custom home, requiring a different approach than purchasing an existing property. Purchasing land involves assessing site development, securing specialized financing, and confirming legal zoning requirements before you commit to a purchase agreement.

According to the National Association of Realtors, vacant land sales often require higher down payments—frequently between 20% and 50%—compared to the 3% to 20% typically required for traditional residential mortgages. Because land does not generate income and cannot be easily liquidated, lenders view it as a higher-risk asset.

What are the different types of land you can buy?

Land is categorized by its level of development, which directly impacts the total cost and timeline of your build. Raw land is completely undeveloped with no access to utilities, roads, or sewage, while improved land features existing connections to municipal infrastructure and necessary permits.

1. Raw land: This is property in its natural state. You will be responsible for clearing trees, grading the earth, and installing every utility, including water, electricity, and septic systems. This is often the cheapest option upfront but the most expensive to prepare. 2. Unimproved land: This property may have some basic infrastructure, such as a driveway or access to a power line, but it lacks essential utilities like water or sewer connections. 3. Improved land: Often called a "build-ready" lot, this land is connected to public utilities, has a driveway, and has been surveyed. While the purchase price is significantly higher, you save on the hidden costs of site development and utility installation.

How do you finance a land purchase?

Financing raw land is distinct from a standard mortgage because there is no structure to serve as collateral for the loan. Most buyers use a land loan, a home construction loan, or seller financing to fund the acquisition.

  • Land loans: These are specialized loans for the purchase of the dirt itself. Because the land is the only collateral, banks often demand a substantial down payment and a higher interest rate than a typical home mortgage.
  • Construction loans: If you intend to build immediately, a construction loan covers both the purchase of the land and the cost of the build. Once the house is complete, the loan typically converts into a standard permanent mortgage.
  • Seller financing: In this arrangement, the seller acts as the bank, allowing you to make payments directly to them. This can be a flexible alternative if you cannot qualify for a traditional bank loan, though it requires a robust legal contract to protect your interest in the property.

What should you check before buying land?

Before closing, you must conduct thorough due diligence to ensure the land is suitable for your specific construction plans. Failing to verify local regulations or environmental conditions can result in a property that is legally impossible to build on.

1. Zoning and land-use restrictions: Check with the local planning department to confirm the land is zoned for residential use. Research any restrictive covenants or homeowners association (HOA) rules that might dictate the size, style, or height of your home. 2. Utility access: If the land is not improved, contact local utility providers to get estimates for bringing water, sewer, electricity, and gas to the site. These "tap fees" can range from several thousand dollars to tens of thousands depending on the distance to the main lines. 3. Soil and environmental testing: A perc test (percolation test) is essential if you need a septic system; it determines how well the soil absorbs water. Additionally, an environmental assessment can reveal if the land has protected wetlands, endangered species habitats, or soil contamination that could halt construction. 4. Survey and boundary verification: Always hire a professional surveyor to mark the exact property lines. Relying on fence lines or old markers can lead to costly legal disputes with neighbors regarding property boundaries.

How do you calculate the total cost of land ownership?

Calculating the true cost of land goes beyond the purchase price. You must account for "soft costs" and "hard costs" that arise before construction even begins. These include property taxes, insurance, permit fees, and the physical labor required to prepare the site for a foundation.

According to the National Association of Home Builders, site preparation costs—including clearing, grading, and utility hookups—can account for 10% to 25% of the total project budget. Always keep a contingency fund of at least 15% of your total budget to cover unexpected site issues, such as hitting bedrock during excavation or discovering a need for specialized drainage systems.

What are the next steps to buying land?

1. Define your budget: Calculate your total construction budget and subtract the estimated cost of the home build to determine your land-buying capacity. 2. Assemble a team: Hire a real estate agent who specializes in land sales, as they understand the nuances of easements, zoning, and rural utility access. 3. Perform due diligence: Never waive your right to a soil test, survey, or utility assessment. Make these items conditions of your purchase contract. 4. Secure your financing: Get pre-approved for a land or construction loan before making an offer to demonstrate your financial viability to the seller.
