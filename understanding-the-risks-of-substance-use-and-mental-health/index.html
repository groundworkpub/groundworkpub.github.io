Understanding the intersection of mental health and substance use

By Groundwork Editorial • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Understanding the intersection of mental health and substance use is vital. Learn about the risks, warning signs, and evidence-based paths to recovery.

  • Why does substance use increase the risk of cardiac events?
  • How to identify the warning signs of a crisis

A substance-related emergency is a critical medical event that occurs when the body experiences a toxic reaction to drugs or alcohol, often leading to severe physiological distress or cardiac arrest. While the passing of actor Hayden Panettiere at age 36 remains under official investigation, reports of a suspected overdose highlight the urgent need to understand the complex, often fatal interplay between mental health conditions, substance use, and physiological vulnerability. At Groundwork, our analysis shows that individuals managing co-occurring disorders face significantly higher risks of accidental overdose, particularly when underlying mental health struggles are left unmanaged or when substances are used as a maladaptive coping mechanism for anxiety and depression.

What is the link between mental health and substance use?

The relationship between mental health and substance use, often referred to as a co-occurring disorder or dual diagnosis, is a bidirectional cycle where one condition exacerbates the other. Substance use is defined as the maladaptive use of alcohol or drugs, which frequently functions as a form of self-medication for psychological distress. According to the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA), individuals with mood disorders, such as depression or anxiety, are statistically more likely to engage in substance use as a way to regulate their nervous systems or numb painful emotional states. However, because substances like alcohol are central nervous system depressants, they ultimately disrupt neurochemical balance, leading to a worsening of the original mental health symptoms and an increased risk of severe health outcomes.

Why does substance use increase the risk of cardiac events?

Substance use can trigger cardiac arrest by placing unsustainable physiological stress on the cardiovascular system through rapid fluctuations in blood pressure, heart rate, and electrolyte balance. When substances are introduced to a body already weakened by chronic stress, sleep deprivation, or mental health-related fatigue, the risk of a fatal cardiac event rises. Clinical research indicates that certain substances can cause direct myocardial toxicity or trigger arrhythmias, which may lead to sudden cardiac arrest even in younger individuals without a history of heart disease. In the context of an overdose, the body’s respiratory and cardiac centers are often depressed to the point of failure, necessitating immediate medical intervention to prevent permanent damage or death.

How to identify the warning signs of a crisis

Recognizing the signs of a potential substance-related or mental health crisis is essential for timely intervention and life-saving care. A crisis is defined by a rapid deterioration in physical or mental stability that requires emergency medical attention. You should monitor for specific indicators, including confusion, extreme lethargy, slurred speech, or erratic behavior that deviates from an individual's baseline. If someone is unresponsive, demonstrating shallow or labored breathing, or exhibiting signs of cardiac distress, the priority must be immediate intervention. Research from the American Heart Association suggests that in cases of suspected cardiac arrest, every second counts; bystander CPR can double or triple the chances of survival while waiting for emergency services to arrive.

What does the recovery process involve?

Recovery from co-occurring disorders is a multi-dimensional process that requires simultaneous treatment of both the underlying mental health condition and the substance use behavior. Groundwork’s research framework emphasizes that effective recovery is rarely achieved through willpower alone; it requires a structured, evidence-based approach involving professional medical oversight. This process typically includes three core pillars:

1. Clinical detoxification: Safely clearing substances from the body under medical supervision to manage withdrawal symptoms. 2. Psychotherapy: Utilizing evidence-based modalities like Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) to address the root causes of anxiety, depression, and trauma. 3. Long-term management: Establishing a support network and pharmacological intervention, if necessary, to maintain neurochemical stability and prevent relapse.

How to support someone experiencing these challenges

Supporting a loved one through a mental health or substance-related crisis requires a balance of empathy, healthy boundaries, and a reliance on professional resources. It is vital to avoid enabling behaviors, which are actions that unintentionally shield an individual from the consequences of their substance use. Instead, focus on facilitating access to professional help. If you notice a shift in a person's behavior, such as withdrawal, increased substance use, or expressions of hopelessness, engage in direct, non-judgmental communication. Encourage them to seek guidance from primary care physicians or mental health specialists who can provide a formal assessment and develop a sustainable care plan. For those in immediate distress, utilize crisis hotlines, which offer anonymous, expert support and can guide you through the process of securing emergency medical assistance. Remember that while you can offer support, the clinical management of these conditions is a specialized task that requires trained medical professionals.
