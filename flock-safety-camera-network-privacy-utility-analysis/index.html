The impact of municipal withdrawal on Flock safety network utility

By Groundwork Editorial • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Discover how municipal exits from the Flock Safety network impact surveillance utility and the critical factors cities must weigh regarding privacy and data.

In This Guide

  • How does the network effect influence ALPR utility?
  • Why are municipalities withdrawing from shared camera contracts?
  • What are the operational risks of private-public surveillance partnerships?
  • How should cities evaluate the efficacy of surveillance investments?

An automated license plate reader (ALPR) network is a system of interconnected cameras that capture vehicle data, such as make, model, and plate numbers, to assist law enforcement in tracking vehicles associated with criminal investigations. Flock Safety, the primary provider of these systems in the United States, operates by creating a shared, collaborative database where participating jurisdictions can view data collected by cameras in other regions.

How does the network effect influence ALPR utility?

The utility of a distributed surveillance network is directly proportional to the density and geographic reach of its camera nodes. At Groundwork, our analysis suggests that the value proposition of a shared network like Flock relies on the 'network effect,' where each additional camera increases the probability of tracking a suspect across municipal borders. When a city removes its cameras, it creates a 'blind spot' in the regional grid, hindering the ability of neighboring jurisdictions to track vehicles moving between those territories.

Data from the Wisconsin municipal exits indicates that the loss of a single hub can degrade the tracking capabilities of a regional network. Law enforcement agencies rely on contiguous data streams to reconstruct travel patterns. If a cluster of cities in a high-traffic corridor opts out, the resulting gaps render the remaining data fragmented and less actionable for criminal investigations. Research suggests that for an ALPR network to maintain high efficacy in identifying suspect vehicles, it requires a near-continuous presence along major arteries, a condition that becomes unsustainable as municipal participation declines.

Why are municipalities withdrawing from shared camera contracts?

Municipalities are increasingly terminating ALPR contracts due to mounting public pressure regarding data privacy, civil liberties, and the lack of transparency surrounding automated surveillance systems. In Wisconsin, the decision by Dane County to terminate its $80,000 investment in Flock cameras underscores a shift in local governance priorities, where the perceived benefits of predictive policing are being weighed against the potential for over-surveillance and the unauthorized storage of citizen movement data.

Privacy advocates and legal experts often cite the 'mission creep' inherent in private-public partnerships as a primary concern. The data collected is not merely used for immediate criminal alerts; it is stored in databases that may remain accessible for extended periods. As cities evaluate these systems, they are scrutinizing the terms of data retention, the criteria for 'hot list' triggers, and the degree of oversight local councils have over private vendors. The trend of withdrawal suggests that when the cost of public trust exceeds the operational utility of the technology, local governments are increasingly opting for removal.

What are the operational risks of private-public surveillance partnerships?

Operating through a private vendor introduces unique risks, including vendor lock-in, proprietary algorithm opacity, and fluctuating service agreements. When a private entity manages the infrastructure, the municipality loses direct control over how the data is processed, analyzed, and shared. At Groundwork, we find that these partnerships often shift the burden of public accountability from the vendor to the local agency, leaving officials to defend technologies they may not fully understand or control.

Furthermore, the reliance on a single vendor creates a systemic vulnerability. If the vendor faces legal challenges, data breaches, or financial instability, the municipal law enforcement apparatus that relies on that specific network faces an immediate loss of capability. Diversifying surveillance infrastructure or opting for more transparent, locally-managed systems is a strategy some municipalities are adopting to mitigate the risks associated with third-party dependency.

How should cities evaluate the efficacy of surveillance investments?

Cities should evaluate ALPR technology by measuring specific, objective outcomes rather than relying on vendor-provided success metrics. Effective evaluation requires a cost-benefit analysis that accounts for the total cost of ownership, including the hidden costs of managing public records requests, the legal costs of privacy lawsuits, and the impact on community relations.

1. Define clear performance metrics: Identify the specific types of crimes the system is intended to solve and establish a baseline for crime reduction without the technology. 2. Conduct a privacy impact assessment: Evaluate how the system stores data, who has access, and whether there are safeguards against improper use. 3. Implement a sunset clause: Require a periodic review of the contract where the city must vote to renew based on demonstrated effectiveness and public feedback. 4. Ensure transparency: Mandate that the vendor provide audit logs and allow for independent oversight of the data management practices.

By following these steps, local governments can ensure that surveillance tools serve the public interest rather than becoming a source of long-term liability. The current trajectory in Wisconsin serves as a case study in how public scrutiny can effectively challenge the unchecked expansion of surveillance networks, forcing a re-evaluation of the balance between security and privacy in the digital age.
