How Jordan’s health system model supports refugees and emergency care

By Dr. Sarah Jenkins • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Learn how Jordan manages a large-scale health system that integrates refugee care, mental health services, and emergency medical evacuations.

Outline

  • Strengthening national health systems through integration
  • The logistics of the medical evacuation corridor
  • Financing and international collaboration
  • Lessons for global health stability
  • Practical next steps for health policy advocacy

A health system model is a framework of organizations, institutions, and resources that work together to improve the health of a population. Jordan’s approach to public health serves as a critical case study for managing large-scale humanitarian crises while simultaneously advancing universal health coverage (UHC). By integrating refugee populations into national health services and maintaining a robust medical corridor for emergency evacuations, Jordan demonstrates how countries can prioritize health as a national security and humanitarian necessity (World Health Organization, 2026).

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), Jordan currently provides health services to more than three million refugees, primarily from Syria and Gaza. This commitment highlights the logistical and financial requirements for hosting displaced populations, a challenge that requires sustained international cooperation and strategic resource allocation.

Strengthening national health systems through integration

Universal health coverage is defined as a system where all people have access to the health services they need, when and where they need them, without financial hardship. Jordan has emerged as a global leader in this area by integrating mental health services directly into primary health care (PHC). Instead of treating mental health as a secondary or specialized concern, the Jordanian model incorporates psychological support into routine medical consultations.

Integrating mental health into primary care reduces the stigma associated with seeking help and ensures that patients receive holistic treatment. The WHO Director-General’s Special Initiative for Mental Health has actively supported these efforts, providing a scalable framework for other nations to follow. By embedding mental health experts within local clinics, the system ensures that trauma and anxiety—common in refugee populations—are addressed early, preventing long-term complications that can overwhelm secondary and tertiary care facilities.

The logistics of the medical evacuation corridor

Emergency medical relief requires more than just clinical expertise; it demands a functional logistics network. Jordan’s Medical Corridor initiative is a prime example of a state-led humanitarian operation that bridges the gap between conflict zones and life-saving treatment. This initiative facilitates the evacuation of ill and injured children from Gaza, ensuring they receive specialized care for cancer, severe trauma, and other life-threatening conditions that cannot be managed in a compromised environment.

To replicate or support such a model, health systems must prioritize the following steps:

1. Establish clear coordination protocols between local hospitals and international aid organizations to ensure seamless patient transfers. 2. Dedicate specialized hospital wings to manage complex, high-acuity cases arriving from conflict zones, ensuring that local health systems are not overly burdened. 3. Implement a multi-stakeholder financing strategy that involves donor countries, UN agencies, and private philanthropies to cover the high costs of specialized emergency equipment and medication. 4. Maintain a robust supply chain for essential medicines to ensure that surge capacity does not result in shortages for the host country’s own citizens.

Financing and international collaboration

Sustaining a health system that supports both its citizens and a massive refugee population requires significant fiscal planning. High-level meetings between Jordanian health officials and international partners focus on "financing priorities," which involve long-term investments rather than short-term emergency grants. The goal is to move from reactive humanitarian aid to proactive health system strengthening.

Data suggests that when international donors invest in the strengthening of a host country's primary health care infrastructure, the return on investment is higher than funding isolated, temporary medical camps. By building capacity within existing Jordanian clinics, international partners help create a resilient network that benefits all residents, regardless of their status. This approach emphasizes that health security is a global collective responsibility.

Lessons for global health stability

Jordan’s strategy offers three core lessons for international health policy. First, political commitment at the highest level is essential. When leadership prioritizes health as a national priority, it mobilizes resources more effectively across government departments. Second, the integration of mental health into primary care is not just a clinical goal but a social one, as it fosters long-term stability for displaced individuals and families.

Third, the "humanity-first" approach to medical care serves as a diplomatic tool that enhances regional stability. By providing care to those in the most vulnerable positions, Jordan has positioned itself as a hub for regional health excellence. This does not mean the system is without strain; rather, it highlights that the sustainability of such models depends entirely on the strength of the partnership between national governments and global health organizations like the WHO.

Practical next steps for health policy advocacy

If you are interested in how these frameworks can be applied to other regions, consider the following actions:

* Research the specific health policy goals of your own region regarding refugee integration and mental health access. * Support advocacy groups that push for the inclusion of mental health services in national health insurance schemes. * Monitor reports from the WHO regarding the Special Initiative for Mental Health to understand how evidence-based frameworks are being translated into local legislation.

By focusing on these structural elements, countries can build systems that are not only equipped to handle routine health needs but are also resilient enough to withstand the pressures of regional crises.
