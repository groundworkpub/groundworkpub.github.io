Cellulose insulation: a guide for modern home builds

By Groundwork Editorial • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Cellulose insulation offers high-density thermal performance and moisture regulation. Learn how to choose between standard and clean fiber for your home.

In This Guide

  • What is the difference between standard and clean fiber cellulose?
  • Why choose cellulose over other insulation materials?
  • How does cellulose perform in wall assemblies?
  • What should you look for in a manufacturer?
  • Is cellulose the right choice for your climate?

Cellulose insulation is a building material made primarily from recycled paper products, such as newspaper or cardboard, treated with fire-retardant chemicals to improve its safety and performance. It is commonly used in residential construction to fill wall cavities, attics, and floor joists to provide thermal resistance and air sealing.

Approximately 80% to 85% of cellulose insulation consists of recycled paper fibers, making it one of the most environmentally friendly insulation options currently available on the market (Source: Department of Energy). When choosing between standard cellulose and "clean fiber" products—which are often marketed as being made from virgin cardboard or specifically processed recycled fibers—it is important to evaluate the chemical composition and the manufacturing stability of the supplier.

What is the difference between standard and clean fiber cellulose?

Standard cellulose is derived from post-consumer recycled newsprint, while clean fiber cellulose typically uses processed cardboard or high-grade paper waste. The primary difference lies in the potential for contaminants and the consistency of the fiber density.

Standard recycled newsprint is the industry workhorse. Manufacturers shred the paper and add borates or ammonium sulfates as fire retardants. Borates are generally preferred in the building science community because they serve as both fire retardants and pest deterrents without the off-gassing concerns sometimes associated with other chemical treatments. Clean fiber products often command a price premium, marketed toward homeowners seeking to avoid the ink residues or potential impurities found in mass-market recycled newsprint. However, provided the product meets ASTM C739 standards, both types offer comparable R-values and fire safety ratings (Source: ASTM International).

Why choose cellulose over other insulation materials?

Cellulose is favored in high-performance building assemblies because of its density and moisture-handling capabilities. Unlike fiberglass batts, which are often loose and prone to convective looping, dense-packed cellulose creates a monolithic barrier that restricts air movement within wall cavities.

One of the most significant advantages of cellulose is its ability to manage moisture. Cellulose fibers are hygroscopic, meaning they can absorb and release small amounts of water vapor without losing their insulating properties or supporting mold growth (Source: Building Science Corporation). This "breathable" characteristic allows the assembly to dry out more effectively than assemblies sealed with rigid foam or non-permeable insulation materials. Furthermore, the borate treatment in cellulose acts as a natural fungicide, further protecting structural wood members from rot in high-humidity climates.

How does cellulose perform in wall assemblies?

When installed as "dense-pack," cellulose is injected into wall cavities at high pressure, reaching a density of approximately 3.5 to 4 pounds per cubic foot. This density is critical for long-term performance because it prevents the material from settling over time.

Proper installation involves: 1. Ensuring the wall cavity is fully enclosed with an air barrier, such as drywall or sheathing. 2. Using a professional-grade blower machine to inject the fibers through small access holes. 3. Verifying the density via a test hole or by measuring the weight of the material used relative to the cavity volume.

When dense-packed, cellulose provides a significant reduction in sound transmission, making it an excellent choice for interior walls or homes located in noisy environments. The material's mass effectively dampens airborne sound frequencies, providing a quieter indoor living space.

What should you look for in a manufacturer?

Selecting a reputable manufacturer is as important as the material itself, as the fire-retardant chemistry determines the long-term safety of your home. You want to ensure the company has a track record of consistent chemical treatment quality.

When vetting a supplier, consider the following steps: 1. Request a technical data sheet (TDS) and verify they are members of the Cellulose Insulation Manufacturers Association (CIMA). 2. Ask for a third-party certification showing compliance with ASTM C739, which covers health, safety, and performance metrics. 3. Check the company’s history of product recalls or supply chain stability. 4. Prioritize manufacturers that use borate-based treatments over those using ammonium sulfates, as borates are more stable and less prone to odor issues in damp conditions.

Is cellulose the right choice for your climate?

In cold climates like Wisconsin, the combination of exterior mineral wool (rockwool) and interior cellulose is considered a high-performance "belt and suspenders" approach. The mineral wool provides a continuous thermal break on the exterior, while the dense-packed cellulose fills the cavity to provide thermal resistance and air sealing.

This hybrid approach leverages the best of both materials: the mineral wool's non-combustibility and water resistance on the exterior, and the cellulose's cost-effectiveness, high-density air sealing, and moisture-buffering capacity on the interior. This combination is highly effective at preventing interstitial condensation, provided the home is designed with proper vapor management and ventilation (Source: Journal of Light Construction).

If you are concerned about the longevity of the manufacturer, focus on companies that have been in the paper-recycling or insulation business for decades. Even if a smaller "clean fiber" startup offers an appealing product, the risk of a warranty claim being honored years down the line is higher if the company lacks a long-standing manufacturing history. Always balance the desire for "cleaner" materials against the proven performance of established, code-compliant industry standards.
