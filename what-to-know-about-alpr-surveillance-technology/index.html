Understanding the impact and risks of automated license plate readers

By Groundwork Editorial • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

Automated license plate readers (ALPRs) track vehicle movements across the US. Learn how this technology works and the privacy risks associated with it.

In This Guide

  • What are automated license plate readers?
  • How does ALPR technology track your movements?
  • What are the primary privacy concerns with ALPR data?
  • Why are some communities removing these cameras?
  • Is there a balance between safety and privacy?

What are automated license plate readers?

Automated license plate readers (ALPRs) are high-speed, computer-controlled camera systems that automatically capture license plate numbers, vehicle descriptions, and timestamps as cars pass by. These systems use artificial intelligence to log vehicle movements into a searchable database, which law enforcement and private security firms use to track location history across cities and regions (The Verge, 2024).

There are currently over 120,000 of these cameras deployed across the United States. While marketed as tools for enhancing public safety and solving crimes, their proliferation has sparked significant debate regarding privacy, data security, and the potential for persistent surveillance of law-abiding citizens.

How does ALPR technology track your movements?

ALPR systems function by scanning every vehicle that enters their field of vision, regardless of whether the driver is suspected of a crime. The software extracts the license plate characters, identifies the vehicle's make, model, and color, and cross-references this data against watchlists or historical logs. This creates a digital breadcrumb trail of where a specific vehicle has been and when it passed through a specific location.

Because these cameras are networked, they do not function in isolation. When multiple cameras are linked, they can effectively map out the travel patterns of individuals, identifying recurring routes to work, school, or medical facilities. This level of granular tracking is what critics argue constitutes a form of mass surveillance that exceeds the traditional scope of police investigation.

What are the primary privacy concerns with ALPR data?

The primary privacy concern with ALPR technology is the lack of strict oversight regarding how long data is stored and who has access to it. Investigative reports have highlighted instances where security flaws left camera feeds exposed to the public internet, potentially allowing unauthorized individuals to view real-time movements (The Verge, 2024). Furthermore, the reliance on third-party AI annotators—sometimes located overseas—to review footage raises questions about data sovereignty and the security of sensitive location information.

Data retention policies vary wildly between jurisdictions. Without clear legal mandates requiring the deletion of data on innocent drivers, this information can reside in databases for months or even years. This creates a "surveillance state" risk where historical travel data can be mined for purposes unrelated to the original intent of the camera installation.

Why are some communities removing these cameras?

Communities are increasingly pushing back against ALPR deployment due to concerns over mission creep and the potential for systemic bias. In cities like Los Angeles, officials have moved to suspend or cancel contracts with providers like Flock Safety, citing the need for better transparency regarding data usage and stronger privacy protections (The Verge, 2024).

Public backlash has also influenced major tech companies. For instance, Amazon-owned Ring recently ended a planned partnership with Flock following significant criticism from privacy advocates who feared the integration would create an inescapable web of private and public surveillance. When deciding whether to support the installation of these cameras in your neighborhood, consider the following steps:

1. Review local city council meeting minutes to see if ALPR contracts are being proposed or renewed. 2. Ask local law enforcement if they have a written policy on data retention and if they share data with federal agencies or private third parties. 3. Demand clear "sunset clauses" in contracts, which require the deletion of non-criminal data within a short, defined period, such as 30 days. 4. Advocate for independent audits of the vendor’s cybersecurity practices to ensure that your location data is not accessible by unauthorized third parties.

Is there a balance between safety and privacy?

Achieving a balance between safety and privacy requires shifting the focus from mass surveillance to targeted investigations. Proponents argue that ALPRs are essential for recovering stolen vehicles and locating missing persons. However, privacy experts suggest that these benefits can be achieved without creating a permanent, searchable log of every citizen's daily life.

If you are concerned about ALPRs in your area, focus your advocacy on requesting "data minimization" policies. This approach limits the collection of information to only what is strictly necessary for public safety, rather than treating every vehicle on the road as a potential data point in a national tracking network.
