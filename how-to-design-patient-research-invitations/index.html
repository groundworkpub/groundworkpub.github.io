A research invitation is the primary document used to recruit patients and public members into clinical studies, serving as the bridge between academic intent and participant engagement. To succeed, these invitations must transition from purely administrative notifications to accessible, persuasive, and clear calls to action that respect the participant's time and perspective.

At Groundwork, our analysis shows that the efficacy of clinical research—specifically Core Outcome Set (COS) studies—hinges on patient participation. When the outcomes measured in a study do not align with what patients value, the resulting evidence often fails to translate into meaningful clinical practice. Improving recruitment starts with the design of the invitation itself.

Why do research invitations often fail to engage patients?

Research invitations frequently fail because they prioritize institutional requirements over participant needs, resulting in language that is overly academic, dense, or disconnected from the patient experience. According to consensus research published in *BMJ Open*, standard invitations often lack the necessary clarity regarding the study's purpose and the individual's role within it, leading to high attrition rates during the recruitment phase.

When invitations are laden with jargon, they create a barrier to entry that disproportionately affects diverse populations and those without a medical background. Groundwork's research framework suggests that an invitation must answer three specific questions within the first two sentences: What is this study about, why does it matter to me, and what is the specific time commitment required? If these are not immediately evident, potential participants are statistically more likely to disengage.

How to structure an effective research invitation

To maximize recruitment, developers should follow a structured approach that emphasizes clarity, accessibility, and relevance. Based on the consensus-driven recommendations for COS studies, you should implement the following five-step design process:

1. **Define the value proposition:** Clearly articulate why the patient’s voice is essential. Explain that their participation will shape future clinical outcomes, rather than simply stating that they are needed for data collection. 2. **Simplify the language:** Use plain language (Grade 8-9 reading level). Avoid acronyms and clinical terminology unless they are explicitly defined in the context of the patient's daily life. 3. **Prioritize layout and formatting:** Use bullet points, bold text for key commitments, and ample white space. Dense paragraphs are the primary cause of reader fatigue in digital research recruitment. 4. **Incorporate user testing:** Before sending the invitation to a wide audience, conduct a pilot test with a small group of patient partners. Ask them to identify what aspects of the invitation feel off-putting or confusing. 5. **Establish accessibility standards:** Ensure the document is compatible with screen readers and available in multiple formats, including large print and digital versions that adapt to mobile devices.

What information must be included to ensure transparency?

Transparency is the foundation of ethical recruitment. A high-quality research invitation must contain a balanced amount of information—providing enough detail for informed consent while avoiding cognitive overload. Research indicates that the most effective invitations provide a clear "roadmap" of the study experience.

At Groundwork, our synthesis of clinical engagement data suggests that participants want to know four specific things before they commit: the nature of the tasks involved, the duration of the participation, the potential impact of their contribution, and the measures taken to protect their privacy. By providing this information upfront, you lower the threshold for participation and increase the likelihood of committed, long-term engagement.

How to reach your target audience effectively

Reaching the right audience requires more than just a well-written document; it requires a strategic distribution plan that meets patients where they are. Relying solely on academic mailing lists often results in a homogenous participant pool. To improve diversity and inclusion, researchers must engage with patient advocacy groups, community centers, and digital health forums.

Data suggests that invitations distributed through trusted community channels see significantly higher conversion rates than those sent via cold outreach. When a patient sees an invitation endorsed or shared by a group they already trust, the perceived legitimacy of the study increases. Furthermore, consider the timing of your outreach; sending invitations during periods of high patient burden or significant life events can decrease response rates.

How to measure the success of your invitation strategy

Success in recruitment is not merely about the number of people who sign up, but the quality and diversity of the participant pool. You should track metrics such as the open rate of your invitation, the conversion rate from "interested" to "enrolled," and feedback from participants regarding the clarity of the initial communication.

At Groundwork, we recommend that researchers treat the recruitment invitation as a dynamic asset. If data shows a high drop-off rate after the initial click, the invitation may be setting expectations that the subsequent study process does not meet. Regularly reviewing these metrics allows you to iterate on your communication strategy, ensuring that you are continuously improving your ability to connect with the patient community.

Actionable next steps for researchers

To improve your current recruitment materials, follow this checklist:

* **Audit your current invitation:** Does it use "we" and "you" language, or does it focus exclusively on the research team's needs? * **Conduct a reading level check:** Use a standard tool to ensure your invitation remains under a 10th-grade reading level. * **Engage a patient partner:** Do not finalize an invitation without feedback from someone who represents the target population. * **Standardize accessibility:** Ensure your document passes WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) standards, as accessibility is a prerequisite for ethical research participation.
