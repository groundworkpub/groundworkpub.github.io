Understanding the implications of the United States withdrawal from the World Health Organization

By Dr. Sarah Jenkins • 4 min read • Updated 2026-08-21

Key Takeaway

A withdrawal from the WHO signifies a shift in global health governance. Understand the legal, financial, and security implications of this transition.

  • The impact on global health security

  • Maintaining scientific integrity in policy

A withdrawal from the World Health Organization (WHO) is a formal exit by a member state from the specialized United Nations agency responsible for international public health. When the United States notifies the WHO of its intent to withdraw, it initiates a complex diplomatic and legal process that alters global health governance, funding structures, and the collaborative framework required to manage transnational health threats. As a founding member, the United States has historically played a central role in shaping the agency's operational priorities and funding mechanisms, according to the WHO official archive (WHO, 2026).

The historical role of the United States in global health

The World Health Organization acts as the directing and coordinating authority on international health work. Since its inception in 1948, the United States has been a primary contributor to the agency’s technical and financial operations. Historical data confirms that U.S. involvement was instrumental in the global eradication of smallpox and has been a cornerstone in combating polio, HIV/AIDS, malaria, and tuberculosis (WHO, 2026). This partnership historically relied on a mutual exchange: the U.S. provided significant financial backing and scientific expertise, while the WHO provided the multilateral platform necessary for coordinating health responses across sovereign borders.

Understanding the withdrawal process

When a member state decides to withdraw, it must adhere to specific constitutional requirements outlined in the WHO Constitution. This typically involves a formal notification period, often spanning one year, during which the withdrawing state must fulfill its outstanding financial obligations. The WHO Executive Board and the World Health Assembly serve as the primary bodies that review these notifications. During these meetings, member states deliberate on the legal and practical ramifications of the exit, including the impact on shared health security initiatives and the potential for future re-engagement (WHO, 2026).

Addressing claims regarding pandemic response

A central component of the U.S. decision to withdraw involves criticism of the WHO’s performance during the COVID-19 pandemic. The U.S. government has characterized the WHO’s actions as insufficient, specifically alleging a lack of transparency regarding the initial spread of the virus. Conversely, the WHO maintains that it acted in accordance with the International Health Regulations. According to WHO records, the agency activated its emergency management system on December 31, 2019, immediately following reports of pneumonia-like symptoms in Wuhan, China (WHO, 2026).

Evidence provided by the WHO indicates that by January 30, 2020—the date the Director-General declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC)—there were fewer than 100 reported cases of COVID-19 outside of China and zero reported deaths globally. The agency argues that its guidance, which focused on mask usage, physical distancing, and vaccination, was evidence-based and advisory in nature, leaving final policy decisions to sovereign governments. This tension highlights the ongoing debate regarding the balance between international coordination and national sovereignty during global crises.

The impact on global health security

Global health security relies on a network of data sharing, laboratory cooperation, and standardized protocols. The withdrawal of a major financial and scientific contributor creates a vacuum in these networks. Without the collaborative structure of the WHO, countries may face increased difficulty in tracking the spread of infectious diseases, coordinating vaccine distribution, and managing antimicrobial resistance. Public health experts note that pathogens do not respect national borders, making multilateral cooperation an essential component of modern disease prevention (WHO, 2026).

Practical steps for navigating global health transitions

For organizations, policymakers, and interested citizens, the withdrawal of a major state actor requires a proactive approach to monitoring global health stability. If you are assessing the impact of this change on health-related sectors, consider the following steps:

1. Monitor official session reports from the World Health Assembly: These documents provide the most accurate account of how the agency plans to reallocate resources and adjust its strategic priorities in the absence of a major member. 2. Review independent audits of international health regulations: Organizations such as the Independent Panel for Pandemic Preparedness and Response offer evidence-based critiques of how multilateral systems can be improved, regardless of member status. 3. Track bilateral health agreements: As multilateral cooperation changes, countries often pivot toward bilateral (country-to-country) health partnerships. Keeping track of these agreements is essential for understanding how the global health landscape is shifting. 4. Evaluate the impact on local health funding: If your work involves public health, ensure that your organization understands which programs were previously supported by the WHO via U.S.-channeled funds and identify alternative sources of support for those initiatives.

Maintaining scientific integrity in policy

Disagreements between nations and international agencies are often rooted in different interpretations of data and governance. However, the core mission of the WHO—to promote health, keep the world safe, and serve the vulnerable—remains a focal point for the remaining member states. Maintaining a focus on peer-reviewed evidence and transparent reporting is critical for ensuring that global health policy remains effective, even during periods of significant geopolitical realignment. While political disagreements are inevitable, the technical work of disease surveillance and eradication remains the primary objective of the WHO’s existence.
