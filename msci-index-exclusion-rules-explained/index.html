How index providers influence corporate asset strategy

What is the role of an index provider?

An index provider is a financial institution that creates and maintains benchmarks used to track the performance of specific market segments, such as global equities or emerging markets. These providers, like MSCI, establish the criteria for which companies are eligible for inclusion in their indexes. When a company is included in a major index, it gains visibility among institutional investors who use these benchmarks to allocate capital, potentially driving up demand for the company’s stock.

Historically, index providers have focused on objective measurements of a company’s operational nature, such as its primary revenue source, market capitalization, and liquidity. However, as corporate treasury strategies evolve to include non-traditional assets, index providers are facing pressure to redefine what constitutes an 'operating company.' This shift has sparked a debate over whether index providers should remain neutral observers of market activity or act as gatekeepers that influence corporate treasury decisions.

Why are companies like Strategy challenging MSCI?

Strategy (formerly MicroStrategy) is challenging MSCI’s proposed methodology for identifying 'non-operating companies' because it believes the criteria unfairly penalize firms for holding specific assets, such as bitcoin. The company argues that index providers should function as neutral measurement tools rather than regulatory bodies that dictate which assets a company is permitted to hold on its balance sheet. According to Strategy, these proposed rules are out of step with current market practices and the evolving nature of digital assets as legitimate corporate treasury holdings (CoinDesk, 2026).

This tension arises because MSCI’s proposed financial-ratio screens could result in the removal of companies from major global equity indexes, such as the MSCI ACWI IMI. If a company is excluded, it may lose access to passive investment capital, as funds tracking those indexes are required to divest from removed stocks. Strategy maintains that it is an operating business—focused on software development and active treasury management—rather than a passive investment vehicle or a digital asset fund.

How do index exclusion rules work?

Index exclusion rules are the specific quantitative and qualitative criteria used by index providers to determine which companies qualify for inclusion in a market index. These rules are designed to ensure that the index accurately reflects the intended market segment, such as the broader stock market or a specific industry sector. When a company fails to meet these criteria, it is typically removed during a scheduled rebalancing period.

1. **Consultation process**: Index providers typically release a proposal for new methodology changes and invite public feedback from market participants, including institutional investors and the companies affected. 2. **Screening criteria**: Providers apply financial ratios, such as the percentage of revenue derived from specific operations or the ratio of non-operating assets to total assets, to categorize a firm. 3. **Rebalancing**: Once the criteria are finalized, the provider reviews the market data, such as the May 2026 data mentioned in MSCI's latest proposal, to identify companies that no longer meet the requirements. 4. **Index adjustment**: Companies that fail the screen are removed from the index, which forces index-tracking funds to sell their shares to maintain alignment with the benchmark (MSCI, 2026).

What are the risks of index-driven corporate governance?

Critics argue that when index providers set strict rules on corporate assets, they effectively impose a form of governance that can restrict a company’s ability to optimize its balance sheet. If a company is forced to choose between its preferred treasury strategy and inclusion in a major index, it may abandon innovative asset management approaches to avoid the negative impact of index exclusion.

This creates a conflict between the index provider’s desire for standardized, predictable index composition and the company’s autonomy to manage its financial resources. As noted by Strategy in its formal objection to MSCI, the 50% asset threshold previously proposed was viewed as an arbitrary metric that failed to account for the actual business operations of the firm. By setting such thresholds, index providers may inadvertently discourage companies from diversifying their holdings into emerging asset classes, even if those assets are sanctioned by regulators.

How should investors evaluate these changes?

Investors should view index methodology changes as a critical factor in understanding the liquidity and institutional support for a stock. When an index provider alters its rules, it can create artificial selling pressure on otherwise healthy companies. To evaluate how these changes might impact your portfolio, consider the following:

* **Analyze the rationale**: Look at the index provider’s stated goal. Is the rule change intended to protect investors from excessive volatility, or is it an attempt to enforce a specific definition of 'operating' business that may be outdated? * **Assess business fundamentals**: Focus on the company’s core revenue generation and operational health rather than its index status. If a company has a robust business model, its long-term value may persist regardless of its inclusion in a specific index. * **Monitor rebalancing dates**: If you hold shares in companies that are at risk of exclusion, be aware that the rebalancing period can lead to short-term price volatility as passive funds shift their holdings.

Ultimately, while index providers provide valuable standardization, their rules are not immutable laws of the market. As the financial landscape changes, the criteria for index inclusion will likely continue to be a point of contention between providers, corporations, and the broader investor community.
